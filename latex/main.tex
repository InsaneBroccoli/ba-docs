\chapter*{Summary}

Diese Bachelorarbeit befasst sich mit der Entwicklung eines Offline-Tuning-Tools für verschiedene Regelkreise von FPV-Drohnen mit Betaflight-Firmware. Ziel des Tools ist es, aufgezeichnete Flugdaten zu analysieren, um die Systemdynamik zu identifizieren und dadurch das Verhalten verschiedener Reglerparameter vorherzusagen, ohne dass für jede Änderung zusätzliche Flugtests erforderlich sind.

Die Messmethode basiert auf der Einbringung eines Chirp-Signals in die zu untersuchenden Regelkreise, wodurch das System über einen breiten Frequenzbereich gezielt angeregt wird. Mithilfe geeigneter Signalverarbeitungstechniken werden die aufgezeichneten Flugdaten ausgewertet, um die Frequenzgänge der Regelkreise zu identifizieren. Auf dieser Grundlage können geeignete Reglerparameter bestimmt werden, um das Flugverhalten zu verbessern.

Die Tuning-Algorithmen für den Winkelgeschwindigkeitsregler und den Winkelregler wurden sowohl in MATLAB als auch in Python implementiert, um eine möglichst breite Zugänglichkeit für die FPV-Community zu gewährleisten. Zusätzlich wurde ein Proof of Concept für die Höhen- und Positionsregelung in MATLAB entwickelt. Diese Untersuchungen sollten sowohl die Anwendbarkeit als auch die Grenzen der Methode für weitere Regelkreise aufzeigen. Aufgrund der laufenden Weiterentwicklung der Betaflight-Firmware wurde auf eine Python-Implementierung dieser Regelkreise verzichtet.

Die Ergebnisse dieser Arbeit werden der Betaflight-Community über ein GitHub-Repository zur Verfügung gestellt und in einem umfassenden Bericht dokumentiert. Die experimentelle Validierung erfolgt durch reale FPV-Flüge, um die Wirksamkeit der vorgeschlagenen Methoden zu überprüfen.

\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Abstract}

This project presents the development of an offline tuning tool for multiple control loops in FPV drones based on the Betaflight firmware. The tool analyzes recorded flight data to estimate the system dynamics and predict the behaviour of different controller parameters without requiring additional flight tests for every parameter change.

The proposed approach is based on the injection of a chirp signal into the control loop, allowing systematic excitation of the system over a wide frequency range. Using signal processing techniques, the recorded data is evaluated to identify the frequency responses of the control loops. Based on these models, suitable controller parameters are derived to improve flight performance.

The tuning algorithms for the angular rate controller and the angle controller are implemented in MATLAB and Python, ensuring accessibility and usability for the FPV community. In addition, prototype implementations for altitude hold and position hold controllers are developed in MATLAB as proof of concept, considering the ongoing development of the Betaflight firmware.

The results of this work are published in a public GitHub repository and documented in a comprehensive report. Experimental validation is performed using real FPV drone flights to verify the effectiveness of the proposed methods.

\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

First-Person-View (FPV) racing drones require precise and responsive control to achieve stable flight behaviour, suppress disturbances such as propwash and maintain agility during rapid maneuvers. The flight performance strongly depends on the tuning of the flight controller’s feedback loops. 
In particular, the parameters of the angular rate controller determine how quickly and accurately the drone reacts to pilot inputs and external disturbances.

In practice, controller tuning is still often performed manually through iterative trial-and-error procedures. This requires extensive flight testing and considerable piloting experience, making the process time-consuming and difficult to reproduce. Although modern flight controller firmware such as Betaflight provides extensive configuration options and detailed flight data logging, systematic tuning remains a challenge.

One commonly used tool for offline tuning is the PID-Toolbox. In this approach, flight logs are recorded during normal flights and later analysed to derive suitable controller parameters. However, the system excitation depends entirely on the pilot’s inputs and the natural flight behaviour of the drone. As a result, not all relevant frequency ranges are sufficiently excited, which can limit the quality of the measurements.

In the approach proposed in this thesis, a chirp excitation signal is injected directly into the control loop. This allows the system to be excited over a wide range of frequencies in a controlled and systematic way, resulting in higher quality measurements for system identification and controller tuning.

A previous project introduced a proof of concept for the offline tuning approach of angular rate controllers based on recorded flight data, originally proposed by our supervisor Michael Peter. In the subsequent project thesis, this concept was restructured and modularized, forming the foundation for the present work.

The objective of this bachelor thesis is to further refine and extend this approach into an offline tuning framework for multiple control loops used in Betaflight-based flight controllers. In addition to the existing angular rate controller tuner, the framework will be extended with code extensions for tuning the angle controller, the altitude controller and the position controller. This extends the tuning framework beyond the inner rate control loop and enables the analysis and tuning of higher-level control layers.

The framework will be implemented primarily in MATLAB and Python and will provide tools for analyzing flight logs, estimating system dynamics and generating controller parameters without requiring repeated flight tests. The software will be developed as an open-source library with accompanying documentation for the FPV community.

By providing a structured, data-driven approach to controller tuning, this project aims to simplify the tuning process and improve flight performance.

\section*{Initial Situation}
\label{sec:IniSituation}

Betaflight is currently the most popular open-source flight controller firmware for FPV racing and freestyle drones and is continuously developed by a large community \parencite{betaflight_website}. The quality of the control algorithms is crucial for flight performance, as it determines how quickly and accurately the drone can respond to pilot inputs and external disturbances.

Currently, controller tuning is often performed manually through iterative trial-and-error procedures or with the support of tools such as the PID Toolbox by Brian White. However, these approaches rely on the natural flight behaviour of the drone and the pilot’s inputs, which may not sufficiently excite all relevant frequency ranges. As a result, this can lead to suboptimal tuning outcomes and requires extensive flight testing as well as significant piloting experience.

During our project thesis, a proof-of-concept approach for offline tuning of the angular rate controller based on recorded flight data, originally developed by Michael Peter, was restructured and modularized. This approach serves as the foundation for the present work and is further refined and extended to include additional control loops.

\section*{Task and Objective}
\label{sec:TaskObjective}

The objective of this bachelor thesis is to further develop and extend the existing offline tuning tool by adding functionality for the tuning of the angle controller. In addition, the tool is to be implemented in Python to improve its accessibility and usability for the FPV community.

Furthermore, proof-of-concept implementations for altitude hold and position hold controllers are developed in MATLAB. These implementations are intended to demonstrate the applicability of the proposed method to additional control loops, while also highlighting its limitations in these contexts.

Due to the ongoing development of the Betaflight firmware and the continuous changes to the corresponding control functionalities, the implementation of these additional controllers in Python was intentionally deferred until a more stable firmware state has been reached.

The results of this work are published in a public GitHub repository and documented in a comprehensive report. The overall goal is to provide a structured, data-driven approach to controller tuning that simplifies the process and improves flight performance for FPV drone pilots.

\section{Drone Types and Sensors}

Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) can generally be classified into different categories, such as fixed-wing, rotary-wing and hybrid systems. This work focuses on multirotor drones, a subgroup of rotary-wing platforms, which generate lift through multiple rotors \parencite{quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017, UAV_hardware}.

Among multirotor UAVs, the most common configurations are quadcopters, hexacopters and octocopters, which differ in the number of rotors (see \autoref{fig:multirotor_types}). Quadcopters use four rotors and represent the simplest fully controllable multirotor configuration. Due to their comparatively low mechanical complexity, good maneuverability and widespread use in research and practical applications, they are particularly relevant for modeling and control tasks \parencite{mahonyMultirotorAerialVehicles2012}.

Hexacopters and octocopters extend this concept by using six or eight rotors. These configurations offer increased thrust, improved fault tolerance and higher payload capacity, but they also involve greater weight, higher energy consumption and more complex control allocation \parencite{mahonyMultirotorAerialVehicles2012}.

Since this work is concerned with the analysis and modeling of quadcopters, the quadcopter configuration is used as the reference platform throughout the remainder of this chapter.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{Figures/multirotor_types.png}
    \caption{Rotary-wing multirotor UAV configurations: quadcopter (left), hexacopter (center) and octocopter (right).}
    \label{fig:multirotor_types}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Sensors}

A quadcopter relies on a variety of sensors to measure its state and environment. The most common sensors, and the most important for this work, are the following \parencite{UAV_hardware, quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017}:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Inertial Measurement Unit (IMU):} The IMU consists of accelerometers and gyroscopes that measure linear acceleration and angular velocity. These measurements are used to estimate the drone's orientation and motion.
    \item \textbf{GPS:} GPS provides position and velocity measurements in a global Earth-fixed reference frame, which are mainly used for navigation and position control.
    \item \textbf{Electronic Speed Controllers (ESCs):} ESCs control the rotational speed of the motors and provide feedback for motor control.
    \item \textbf{Magnetometer:} A magnetometer measures the Earth's magnetic field and is used to estimate the heading of the drone.
    \item \textbf{Barometer:} A barometer measures atmospheric pressure, which can be used to estimate altitude.
\end{itemize}

These sensors provide the measurements needed for offline tuning. Depending on the application, additional sensors such as cameras or distance sensors may be used. In practice, measurements from multiple sensors are often combined through sensor fusion to obtain a more accurate estimate of the drone’s state. Sensor data form the basis for stabilization, navigation and control \parencite{quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017}.

\section{Drone Dynamics and Control Principles}

A quadcopter is a dynamic system that uses four rotors to generate lift and control its motion. By adjusting the rotational speeds of the individual motors, the drone can control its orientation and movement in three-dimensional space.

In multicopters, all translational and rotational forces are generated by the individual propellers. Stable flight therefore requires continuous feedback control to maintain the desired orientation and compensate for disturbances.

In this work, the focus lies on the rotational dynamics of the drone, which are described by the three degrees of freedom: roll, pitch and yaw. These rotational motions are controlled by varying the thrust of the individual motors.

The following sections introduce the coordinate system, the propeller configuration and the motor mixing approach, which together form the basis for the control strategy used in this work.

\subsection*{Coordinate System and Rotational Axes}

The quadcopter's orientation is described using a body-fixed coordinate system with three rotational degrees of freedom (see \autoref{fig:body_axes}), following the right-hand rule:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Roll} (\(\phi\)): rotation around the forward-pointing \(x\)-axis
    \item \textbf{Pitch} (\(\theta\)): rotation around the rightward-pointing \(y\)-axis
    \item \textbf{Yaw} (\(\psi\)): rotation around the downward-pointing \(z\)-axis
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.52\linewidth]{body_axes.png}
    \caption{Body-fixed coordinate system and rotational axes of a quadcopter. \parencite{autonomous_robots} }
    \label{fig:body_axes}
\end{figure}

All motion is controlled by adjusting the rotational speeds of the four motors, which generate the necessary forces and torques. The motor configuration and control strategy are described in the following sections \parencite{autonomous_robots, quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017, mahonyMultirotorAerialVehicles2012}.

\newpage

\subsection*{Force and Torque Generation}

As already mentioned, a quadcopter generates all forces and torques through the thrust produced by the propellers. Therefore, the control of the drone is achieved by adjusting the speed of each motor, which in turn changes the thrust and the resulting forces and torques.

Rolling the drone is achieved by increasing the speed of two motors on one side and decreasing the speed of the opposite motors. For example, to roll the drone to the left, the speeds of the right motors are increased while the speeds of the left motors are decreased. This creates a torque around the \(x\)-axis, causing the drone to tilt, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:roll_pitch}. The same principle also applies to pitch control, where the front and rear motors are adjusted to create a torque around the \(y\)-axis.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \begin{minipage}{0.48\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[height=6.4cm]{Drone_Roll.png}
        \caption{Roll and pitch control through differential motor speeds.}
        \label{fig:roll_pitch}
    \end{minipage}
    \hfill
    \begin{minipage}{0.48\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[height=6.4cm]{Drone_yaw.png}
        \caption{Yaw control through differential motor speeds.}
        \label{fig:yaw}
    \end{minipage}
\end{figure}

Translational motion along the \(x\)- and \(y\)-axes is achieved by tilting the airframe, thereby redirecting a component of the total thrust vector horizontally. Forward motion requires a positive pitch angle \(\theta > 0\), generated by increasing the rear motor speeds and reducing the front motor speeds. Lateral motion works the same way, using a roll angle (\(\phi \neq 0\)) created by a speed difference between the left and right motor pairs. As a result, motion in the \(x\)- and \(y\)-directions is coupled to the roll and pitch angles \(\phi\) and \(\theta\).

Yaw control relies on the reaction torques produced by the propellers. Since adjacent motors spin in opposite directions, their drag torques cancel out under symmetric operation. By speeding up the motors rotating in one direction and slowing down those rotating in the other, a net torque about the \(z\)-axis is generated, which causes the drone to rotate in the desired direction, as shown in \autoref{fig:yaw} \parencite{autonomous_robots}.

Combining these principles, all motions are controlled through the four motor speeds \(\Omega_1, \Omega_2, \Omega_3, \Omega_4\). The process of mapping the desired roll, pitch, yaw and throttle commands to individual motor speed setpoints is called \textit{control allocation} or \textit{motor mixing} and is a core part of the flight controller. For a standard quadcopter, this mapping is given by the following equations:

\begin{align}
    M_1 &= T + \phi + \theta - \psi, \\
    M_2 &= T - \phi + \theta + \psi, \\
    M_3 &= T - \phi - \theta - \psi, \\
    M_4 &= T + \phi - \theta + \psi,
\end{align}

where \(M_i\) represents the speed command for the \(i\)-th motor, \(T\) is the throttle command and \(\phi\), \(\theta\) and \(\psi\) are the desired roll, pitch and yaw commands, respectively. The specific coefficients in these equations depend on the configuration of the drone and the direction of rotation of the motors \parencite{quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017, mahonyMultirotorAerialVehicles2012}.

The general structure of the flight control system is illustrated in \autoref{fig:control_loop}. It shows how the desired maneuver (setpoint) is processed by the controller, which computes motor commands to influence the drone. The resulting motion is measured by sensors and fed back into the system, forming a closed control loop.

The flight control system consists of multiple control loops that regulate the drone's behaviour. These control loops are the primary subject of this work and will be analysed and tuned in the following sections \parencite{quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017}.

\section{System Identification Using Chirp Signals}
\label{sec:System Identification Using Chirp Signals}

In this chapter, the theoretical background of the approach used for the estimation and calculation of the different control loops is described. Since the fundamental approach of all the control loops is the same, the general approach is described here and the specific details of each control loop are described in the Methodology chapter.

To support the theoretical concepts, a simplified simulation model is added. The simulation is gradually developed throughout this chapter, allowing the reader to follow the modelling process step by step and to better understand the behaviour of the control system. This approach should provide a clear and practical understanding of the control loops and the signal processing techniques used in the estimation and calculation of the control loops.

The detailed application of these principles to the different control loops implemented in Betaflight is discussed in the following chapter.

\subsection*{Chirp Signal}

A chirp signal is a signal in which the frequency increases or decreases over time. It is commonly used in system identification and control engineering to analyze the response of a system over a range of frequencies \parencite{Matlab_Chirp}.

The main advantage of a chirp signal is that it allows a wide frequency range to be excited within a single experiment. Instead of testing individual frequencies one by one, the system is continuously stimulated with increasing frequency. This makes the approach both efficient and well suited for practical applications.

By observing how the system responds to this input, it becomes possible to analyse its frequency-dependent behaviour. In particular, the measured input and output signals can be used to estimate the frequency response of the system. This provides valuable insight into the system dynamics and forms the basis for the methods described in the following sections \parencite{Matlab_Chirp}.

\newpage

\subsubsection*{Mathematical Definition}

The instantaneous value of the chirp signal is defined as

\begin{equation}
    x(t) = A \cdot \sin(\varphi(t)),
\end{equation}

where \( A \) is the amplitude, which determines the excitation strength and therefore 
influences the signal-to-noise ratio of the measurement \parencite{Matlab_Chirp}.

The phase \( \varphi(t) \) represents the accumulated instantaneous frequency over time  and is defined as

\begin{equation}
    \varphi(t) = 2\pi \int_0^t f(\tau)\, d\tau,
\end{equation}

where \( f(\tau) \) denotes the instantaneous frequency at time \( \tau \). The specific evolution of \( f(\tau) \) over time is discussed in the following sections \parencite{Matlab_Chirp}.

In practical implementations such as \textbf{Betaflight}, the phase is wrapped once it reaches \( 2\pi \), keeping the numerical values bounded and producing a sawtooth-shaped phase signal that serves as the argument of the sinusoidal excitation.

The quality of the chirp excitation depends strongly on the chosen sweep parameters, including the frequency range, sweep duration, sampling frequency and amplitude. The following parameter values are used in the simulation model of this work:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \( f_0 \) Starting frequency of the chirp signal: \( 1\,\text{Hz} \)
    \item \( f_1 \) Final frequency of the chirp signal: \( 50\,\text{Hz} \)
    \item \( T \) Total duration of the frequency sweep: \( 20\,\text{s} \)
    \item \( f_s \) Sampling frequency: \( 2000\,\text{Hz} \)
    \item \( A \) Amplitude of the chirp signal: \( 230 \)
\end{itemize}

With the exception of \( f_1 \), these values are taken from the default chirp settings used in Betaflight for the angle and gyro control loops. The final frequency in the simulation is limited to \( 50\,\text{Hz} \) to improve plot readability, whereas in practice \( f_1 \) is typically set much higher (e.g., \( 600\,\text{Hz} \)) to cover the full control bandwidth of the system.

\newpage

\subsubsection*{Exponential chirp:}

For an exponential chirp, the instantaneous frequency increases exponentially from the starting frequency \( f_0 \) to the final frequency \( f_1 \) over the time interval \( T \). This means that lower frequencies are covered more slowly, while higher frequencies are reached more rapidly. \parencite{Matlab_Chirp}

The instantaneous frequency is defined as

\begin{equation}
    f(t) = f_0 \left(\frac{f_1}{f_0}\right)^{\frac{t}{T}}.
\end{equation}

Compared to the linear chirp, this results in a more uniform excitation in logarithmic
frequency space, which is particularly useful when analysing systems using Bode plots \parencite{Matlab_Chirp}.

The corresponding phase trajectory is obtained by integrating the frequency 

\begin{equation}
    \varphi(t) = \frac{2\pi T f_0}{\ln(f_1/f_0)}
    \left[
    \left(\frac{f_1}{f_0}\right)^{\frac{t}{T}}-1
    \right].
\end{equation}

These expressions define the phase trajectory \( \varphi(t) \) used both in the simulations and in the time-dependent frequency shifting approach. \autoref{fig:exponential_chirp} shows the resulting exponential chirp signal together with its instantaneous frequency and phase trajectory \parencite{Matlab_Chirp}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{Figures/exponential_chirp.png}
    \caption{Exponential chirp signal used for the simulation. The upper plot shows the generated chirp signal, the middle plot the exponential frequency sweep and the lower plot the wrapped phase used in the signal generation.}
    \label{fig:exponential_chirp}
\end{figure}



\subsubsection*{Lag Filter for Chirp Input Shaping}

In some control loops, the chirp signal is conditioned by a first-order lag filter before being injected into the control loop. The controller effort magnitude tends to increase towards higher frequencies, which may lead to excessive motor response during the high-frequency portion of the sweep and can compromise the quality of the system identification data. The shaping filter therefore attenuates the high-frequency components of the chirp signal prior to injection, resulting in a more uniform excitation across the frequency range.

By default, the filter is implemented as a first-order lead--lag element with a pole at \( f_p = 3\,\mathrm{Hz} \) and a zero at \( f_z = 30\,\mathrm{Hz} \), corresponding to the transfer function

\begin{equation}
    H(s) = \frac{1 + s/\omega_z}{1 + s/\omega_p},
\end{equation}

where \( \omega_z = 2\pi f_z \) and \( \omega_p = 2\pi f_p \). Since \( f_p < f_z \), the filter exhibits an overall lag characteristic. Its gain is approximately one at low frequencies and approaches a constant attenuation of \( \omega_p/\omega_z = f_p/f_z \) at high frequencies.

For newer developments, the filter parameters are optimized based on the measured controller effort. The objective is to reduce excitation in frequency ranges where the controller effort is high and increase it where the controller effort is low, resulting in a more uniform excitation of the system. \autoref{fig:Lag_Result} provides an illustrative continuous-time visualization of this shaping concept. It shows how the lag filter compensates the rising magnitude of the controller effort, such that the product of both responses is approximately flat across the relevant frequency range.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{SC_LAG_LEAD_Result.png}
    \caption{Magnitude response of the controller effort (black), the lag filter (red) and their combined result (blue) for the simulation parameters \( f_p = 3\,\text{Hz} \), \( f_z = 30\,\text{Hz} \). The combined response is approximately flat across the relevant frequency range.}
    \label{fig:Lag_Result}
\end{figure}

In the Betaflight implementation, the pole frequency \( f_p \) must not exceed the zero frequency \( f_z \). Otherwise, the element would become a lead filter with increasing gain towards high frequencies, which could amplify high-frequency excitation and potentially overstress the motors.

For the simulation shown in \autoref{fig:chirpleadlag}, the same parameters as in the previous section were used (\(f_0 = 1\,\mathrm{Hz}\), \(f_1 = 50\,\mathrm{Hz}\), \(T = 20\,\mathrm{s}\), \(f_s = 2000\,\mathrm{Hz}\)). The excitation signal is generated using the exponential chirp formulation introduced earlier and subsequently shaped by the lag filter.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{chirp_lag_filtert.png}
    \caption{Exponential chirp signal after conditioning with the lag filter used for chirp input shaping.}
    \label{fig:chirpleadlag}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Signal Processing}

To illustrate the influence of the applied signal-processing steps, a simulation is used as a reference. The simulated system represents a simplified model of the plant of the drone's angular-rate controller. It consists of an integrator, a low-pass filter and a dead-time component. These elements capture the main dynamics of the system and are commonly used in control engineering.

The overall transfer function of the system is given by

\begin{equation}
    G(s) = \frac{\omega_1}{s} \cdot \frac{1}{1 + s/\omega_2} \cdot e^{-\frac{s}{\omega_t}}.
\end{equation}

The integrator describes how differences in rotor thrust affect the angular rate over time. The first-order term represents the low-pass behaviour introduced by the electronics and actuator dynamics. The dead-time component accounts for delays in the control loop.

For the simulation, the parameters are chosen as

\begin{equation}
    \omega_1 = 16 \cdot 2\pi, \qquad
    \omega_2 = 13 \cdot 2\pi, \qquad
    \omega_t = 60 \cdot 2\pi.
\end{equation}

A block diagram of the simulation model is shown in \autoref{fig:signal_processing_model}.

\subsubsection*{Baseband Filtering via Frequency Rotation}

Measured signals are often affected by noise and unwanted high-frequency components, which can obscure relevant information. This is also the case in the simulated signals considered here, where additive noise is present. To reduce these disturbances while preserving the phase characteristics, a frequency-shifting filtering approach is used.

The method shifts the signal to baseband, applies a low-pass filter and then shifts it back to its original frequency range, enabling focused filtering around the frequency of interest.

The underlying principle can be understood using the Fourier shift theorem. Multiplication of a signal $f(t)$ with a complex exponential results in a shift of its spectrum

\begin{equation}
    e^{i2\pi\xi_0 t} f(t)
    \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
    \hat{f}(\xi-\xi_0),
\end{equation}

which shifts the spectrum by the frequency $\xi_0$ \parencite{sisy_skript}.

In this case, the signal of interest is a chirp signal with a continuously changing instantaneous frequency. Therefore, the frequency shift has to be time-dependent. This is achieved using a complex phasor

\begin{equation}
    p(t)=e^{i\varphi(t)},
\end{equation}

where \( \varphi(t) \) describes the instantaneous phase of the chirp. Multiplying the measured signal $x(t)$ with this phasor or its complex conjugate results in a rotation of the signal spectrum toward baseband \parencite{signal_processing}

\begin{equation}
    y_R(t)=x(t)p(t), \qquad
    y_Q(t)=x(t)p^*(t).
\end{equation}

After this rotation, the dominant signal component is centered around $0\,\text{Hz}$, enabling efficient filtering using a low-pass filter.

Once the signal has been shifted to baseband, a low-pass filter is applied to remove high-frequency noise components. Since the useful signal is concentrated near zero frequency, the filter can effectively suppress unwanted spectral components outside this region. To avoid phase distortion, forward-backward filtering is employed. This approach applies the filter in both forward and reverse directions, resulting in zero-phase filtering. The filtering operation yields the filtered baseband signals

\begin{equation}
    y_R(t) \rightarrow \tilde{y}_R(t), \qquad
    y_Q(t) \rightarrow \tilde{y}_Q(t).
\end{equation}

After filtering, the signal is shifted back to its original frequency range. This is achieved by multiplying the filtered signals with the corresponding inverse phasors and combining the results to obtain a real-valued signal \parencite{signal_processing}

\begin{equation}
    x_f(t) = \mathrm{Re}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left(\tilde{y}_R(t)p^*(t)+\tilde{y}_Q(t)p(t)\right)\right).
\end{equation}

This step reverses the initial frequency rotation and reconstructs a filtered version of the original signal. By reducing the noise before the spectral estimation, the signal-to-noise ratio is improved, leading to more reliable results \parencite{signal_processing}.

A second-order Butterworth low-pass filter with a cutoff frequency of $10\,\text{Hz}$ is used. The effect of the filtering approach is illustrated in \autoref{fig:time_filtered}. At higher chirp frequencies, noise dominates the unfiltered signal, while the filtered signal closely follows the underlying system response. A slight amplitude reduction at higher frequencies can be observed, consistent with the low-pass behaviour.

\end{figure}

\subsubsection*{Frequency Response Estimation}

For a linear time-invariant (LTI) system, the input-output relationship in the frequency domain can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
    Y(\omega) = G(\omega)U(\omega),
\end{equation}

where \(U(\omega)\) and \(Y(\omega)\) denote the Fourier transforms of the input signal \(u(t)\) and the output signal \(y(t)\), respectively and \(G(\omega)\) represents the frequency response of the system.

To estimate the frequency response from measured signals, both sides of the equation are multiplied by the complex conjugate \(U^{H}(\omega)\) of the input signal,

\begin{equation}
    Y(\omega)U^{H}(\omega) = G(\omega)U(\omega)U^{H}(\omega).
\end{equation}

Using the estimates

\begin{equation}
    \hat{S}_{yu}(\omega) = Y(\omega)U^{H}(\omega)
\end{equation}

for the cross-power spectral density between the output and input signals and

\begin{equation}
    \hat{S}_{uu}(\omega) = U(\omega)U^{H}(\omega)
\end{equation}

for the auto-power spectral density of the input signal, the frequency response can be estimated as

\begin{equation}
    \hat{G}(\omega) = \frac{\hat{S}_{yu}(\omega)}{\hat{S}_{uu}(\omega)}.
\end{equation}

This equation forms the basis for the frequency response estimation used throughout this work. Compared to directly dividing the Fourier transforms, the use of spectral density estimates leads to more robust results in the presence of measurement noise. Since the excitation signal is known and measurement noise is mainly present in the system output, this approach is well suited for system identification.

In practice, the spectral densities are estimated from finite measurement records. To obtain reliable estimates, averaging techniques are required to reduce the influence of noise and random variations. In this work, the spectral density estimation is performed using Welch's method, which is introduced in the following section.


\subsubsection*{Spectral Estimation Using Welch’s Method}

To obtain reliable estimates of the spectral densities, Welch’s method is applied. As discussed in the previous section, direct computation from finite data is sensitive to noise. Therefore, averaging techniques are required to approximate the expected values of the spectral quantities \parencite{welchUseFastFourier1967, System_Identification}. 

The spectral densities describe how the signal energy is distributed over frequency and form the basis for estimating the frequency responses \parencite{System_Identification}.

Let \(u[n]\) and \(y[n]\) denote the discrete-time input and output signals of length \(N\). In Welch’s method, the signals are divided into \(K\) overlapping segments of length \(L\). Each segment is multiplied by a window function \(w[n]\) to reduce spectral leakage \parencite{System_Identification}.

The \(k\)-th segment of the input signal is given by

\begin{equation}
    u_k[n] = u[n + kD]\, w[n], \qquad n = 0, \dots, L-1,
\end{equation}

where \(D\) defines the shift from one segment to the next. It therefore determines how many samples are shared between neighbouring segments \parencite{System_Identification}. For each segment, the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) is computed as

\begin{equation}
    U_k(\omega) = \sum_{n=0}^{L-1} u_k[n] e^{-j\omega n}, \qquad
    Y_k(\omega) = \sum_{n=0}^{L-1} y_k[n] e^{-j\omega n}.
\end{equation}

To obtain a stable representation in the frequency domain, the squared magnitude of the Fourier transform is used. This corresponds to the signal power and makes the spectral estimate less sensitive to noise \parencite{System_Identification}. The periodogram estimates are defined as

\begin{equation}
    P_{uu,k}(\omega) = \frac{1}{L} U_k(\omega) \cdot U_k^{H}(\omega), \qquad
    P_{yu,k}(\omega) = \frac{1}{L} Y_k(\omega) U_k^{H}
\end{equation}

Welch’s method reduces the variance of the estimates by averaging over all segments. The resulting spectral densities are given by

\begin{equation}
    \hat{S}_{uu}(\omega) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} P_{uu,k}(\omega), \qquad
    \hat{S}_{yu}(\omega) = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{k=1}^{K} P_{yu,k}(\omega), 
\end{equation}

Using these quantities, the frequency response estimate is computed as

\begin{equation}
    \hat{G}(\omega) = \frac{\hat{S}_{yu}(\omega)}{\hat{S}_{uu}(\omega)}.
\end{equation}

In our example, the spectral densities are estimated using Welch’s method with a segment length of \(L = 4096\) samples and an overlap of 90\%. A Hanning window is applied to each segment to minimize spectral leakage.

\newpage

The estimated frequency response is shown in \autoref{fig:transfer_estimation_filtered}. The estimated magnitude and phase closely follow the true system response over a wide frequency range. Compared to the direct estimation approach, the usable frequency range is significantly extended.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{transferfunction_after_processing.png}
    \caption{Comparison of the true and estimated frequency responses obtained using Welch’s method. The magnitude response is shown at the top, the phase response in the middle and the coherence at the bottom.}
    \label{fig:transfer_estimation_filtered}
\end{figure}


The quality of the frequency response estimate can be evaluated using the coherence. As already mentioned, coherence values close to one indicate a reliable frequency response estimate. The coherence is defined as

$$
\gamma^2(\omega) = \frac{|S_{yu}(\omega)|^2}{S_{uu}(\omega)\, S_{yy}(\omega)},
$$

and takes values in the range \(0 \leq \gamma^2(\omega) \leq 1\). It provides a frequency-dependent measure of how strongly the output is linearly related to the input \parencite{signal_processing}.

As shown in \autoref{fig:transfer_estimation_filtered}, the coherence remains close to one up to approximately 10 Hz, where only minor deviations between the estimated and true frequency responses are visible. At higher frequencies, the coherence decreases and the estimation error gradually increases.

\section{Control Loop Calculations}
\label{sec:Control Loop Calculations}

Although the flight controller consists of several nested control loops, their fundamental structure is identical. Each loop follows the same basic feedback control principle and differs mainly in the controlled variable and the chosen controller parameters. Therefore, instead of analysing every loop individually from the beginning, a simplified model is used to explain the underlying calculations and controller behaviour.

The simplified control structure used for the analysis consists of a controller \(C\) and a plant \(P\), which describes the dynamic behaviour of the system being controlled. The reference input \(r\) is compared with the system output \(y\) to form the control error \(e\). Based on this error, the
controller generates the control signal \(u\), which drives the plant. In addition, the model considers both input and output disturbances, allowing a more realistic representation of the system behaviour, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:control_loop_easy}.

Using this simplified model allows the general behaviour of the control loop and the calculation of the controller terms to be explained in a clear and structured way. The same principles are later applied to the individual control loops implemented in the flight controller.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Control_Loop_easy.png}
    \caption{Simplified feedback control loop with controller \(C\) and plant \(P\), including reference \(r\), error \(e\), control signal \(u\) and input/output disturbances.}
    \label{fig:control_loop_easy}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Calculation of the Control Loop Components}

After the signal processing steps, the control loop components can be calculated. As in the signal processing stage, the goal is to mitigate the influence of disturbances and noise on the estimated control loop components. Therefore, only the input signal of the control loop is used for the estimation.

The advantage of this approach is twofold:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The input signal is not affected by the back-calculation within the control loop and is therefore not influenced by disturbances or noise present inside the loop.
    \item The input signal is not subject to filtering effects, since it is not measured but taken directly from the software. Consequently, it is free from measurement noise and external disturbances.
\end{itemize}

This approach enables a consistent estimation of the transfer function. However, it also implies that the transfer functions of the individual control loop components cannot be obtained directly. Instead, they must be derived through mathematical manipulations.

\newpage

Consider a simplified control loop consisting of a controller \( C(s) \) and a plant \( P(s) \), as illustrated in \autoref{fig:control_loop_easy}. The closed-loop transfer function from the reference input \( R(\omega) \) to the output \( Y(\omega) \) is given by \parencite{lutzTaschenbuchRegelungstechnik}

\begin{equation}
    G_{yr}(\omega) = \frac{Y(\omega)}{R(\omega)} =
    \frac{C(\omega)P(\omega)}{1 + C(\omega)P(\omega)}= T(\omega).
    \label{eq:Gyr}
\end{equation}

The transfer function from the reference input to the controller output \( U(\omega) \) is

\begin{equation}
    G_{ur}(\omega) = \frac{U(\omega)}{R(\omega)} =
    \frac{C(\omega)}{1 + C(\omega)P(\omega)} = SC(\omega).
    \label{eq:Gur}
\end{equation}

By taking the ratio of these two transfer functions, the plant transfer function can be determined:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{G_{yr}(\omega)}{G_{ur}(\omega)} = \frac{\dfrac{C(\omega)P(\omega)}{1 + C(\omega)P(\omega)}}
    {\dfrac{C(\omega)}{1 + C(\omega)P(\omega)}} = P(\omega).
    \label{eq:Plant}
\end{equation}

To determine the controller, the same principle is applied. The controller transfer function can be calculated as

\begin{equation}
    \frac{G_{ur}(\omega)}{1-G_{yr}(\omega)} = \frac{\dfrac{C(\omega)}{1 + C(\omega)P(\omega)}}
    {1 - \dfrac{C(\omega)P(\omega)}{1 + C(\omega)P(\omega)}} = C(\omega).
\end{equation}

These relationships enable the identification of both the plant and the controller from measurable closed-loop signals. They provide the theoretical
foundation for the analysis of the individual control loops.

\newpage

\subsection*{Closed-Loop Performance Metrics}

After determining the plant transfer function, the next step is to analyse the behaviour of the closed-loop system. This is achieved by evaluating several closed-loop transfer functions, which provide information about tracking performance, robustness, disturbance rejection and controller effort. \parencite{lutzTaschenbuchRegelungstechnik}

The closed-loop transfer function from the reference input to the output is given by \parencite{lutzTaschenbuchRegelungstechnik}

\begin{equation}
    T(\omega) = \frac{C(\omega)P(\omega)}{1 + C(\omega)P(\omega)}.
\end{equation}

The transfer function \(T(\omega)\) describes how well the system follows the reference input and is therefore often referred to as the tracking transfer function.

The sensitivity function is defined as \parencite{lutzTaschenbuchRegelungstechnik}

\begin{equation}
    S(\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + C(\omega)P(\omega)}.
\end{equation}

It describes the sensitivity of the system to disturbances and modelling errors and is commonly used as a measure of robustness. Small values indicate good disturbance attenuation, whereas large peaks indicate reduced robustness.

The compliance function is given by \parencite{lutzTaschenbuchRegelungstechnik}

\begin{equation}
    SP(\omega) = P(\omega)\,S(\omega).
\end{equation}

It describes how disturbances acting on the plant propagate to the system output and therefore characterizes the disturbance rejection capability of the closed-loop system.

Finally, the controller effort is defined as \parencite{lutzTaschenbuchRegelungstechnik}

\begin{equation}
    SC(\omega) = C(\omega)\,S(\omega).
\end{equation}

This transfer function describes how strongly the controller reacts to reference changes. Excessively large values may lead to actuator saturation or amplified measurement noise.

\subsection*{Compensation of the I-Term}

The integral term of a PI or PID controller is used to compensate for steady-state errors by integrating the control error over time. In flight control systems, this helps to counteract aerodynamic effects. During fast stick movements or aggressive maneuvers, however, the integral term can accumulate excessive error. As a result, the accumulated I-term no longer represents a disturbance but instead opposes the intended motion, which can lead to overshoot, bounce-back effects, or slow settling after the maneuver \parencite{RT1_Skript}.

To reduce these effects, Betaflight uses a feature called I-Term Relax \parencite{I_Term}. This mechanism temporarily reduces the integral action when rapid changes are detected. Depending on the configuration, these changes are identified either from the control setpoint or from the measured gyroscope signal. When a defined threshold is exceeded, the I-term integration is reduced or paused. This helps to prevent excessive buildup of the integral term during aggressive maneuvers, reducing overshoot and bounce-back while maintaining good disturbance rejection.

For analysis and controller tuning, the effect of I-Term Relax must be considered, as it modifies the controller behaviour depending on the current flight conditions.
\begin{figure}[h]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Control_Loop_Iterm.png}
	\caption{Simplified closed-loop control system including the I-Term Relax mechanism, where \(r\) is the reference signal, \(e\) the control error, \(u\) the controller output and \(y\) the system output.}
	\label{fig:I_term}
\end{figure}

The I-Term Relax algorithm is inherently nonlinear because the integral action is modified depending on the current flight condition. As a result, the effective controller behaviour changes over time and cannot be fully represented by a fixed linear transfer function. To account for this effect, the measured controller transfer function is compared with an analytically derived controller transfer function. A compensation factor \( C_{\mathrm{PI,com}}(\omega) \) is then defined as the ratio between the measured PI controller \( C_{\mathrm{PI}}(\omega) \) transfer function and its analytical counterpart \( C_{\mathrm{PI,ana}} \):

\begin{equation}
	C_{\mathrm{PI,com}}(\omega) = \frac{C_{\mathrm{PI}}(\omega)}{C_{\mathrm{PI,ana}}(\omega)}
\end{equation}

This frequency-dependent compensation factor represents the combined influence of effects that are not explicitly modeled, including the impact of I-Term Relax. Applying this factor scales the controller transfer function at each frequency such that the measured controller behaviour is reproduced. In this way, the effect of I-Term Relax can be approximated without explicitly modeling the nonlinear algorithm. The factor is further applied to both the original analytical PI controller \( C_{\mathrm{PI,ana}} \) and the newly designed PI controller \( C_{\mathrm{PI,new}} \):

\begin{align}
	C_{\mathrm{PI,ana,com}}(\omega) & = C_{\mathrm{PI,ana}}(\omega) \cdot C_{\mathrm{PI,com}}(\omega) \\
	C_{\mathrm{PI,new,com}}(\omega) & = C_{\mathrm{PI,new}}(\omega) \cdot C_{\mathrm{PI,com}}(\omega)
\end{align}

The resulting compensated controller transfer functions are then reinserted into the closed-loop system for further analysis and evaluation.

\subsection*{Step Response Analysis}

The step response provides a direct and intuitive way to evaluate the performance of a control system. By applying a step input to the reference signal, the resulting output response can be analysed with respect to key performance metrics such as rise time, overshoot and settling time. These metrics provide insight into the speed, stability and accuracy of the control system. Therefore, the step response is a simple and practical method for evaluating controller settings, especially for users without a deeper background in control engineering.

The first step in calculating the step response is to transform the frequency response of the system back into the time domain. This is done using the inverse fast Fourier transform (IFFT). The resulting signal corresponds to the impulse response of the system

\begin{equation}
g[k] = \mathrm{IFFT}\left\{T(\omega)\right\}.
\end{equation}

For the step response, a unit step is used as the input signal. The reference signal can therefore be written as

\begin{equation}
r[k] = 1 \qquad \text{for } k \geq 0.
\end{equation}

The step response is obtained by combining the impulse response with the unit step input. In discrete time, this can be written as

\begin{equation}
y[k] = \sum_{i=0}^{k} g[i] \cdot r[i].
\end{equation}

Since the value of the unit step remains equal to one after the step occurs, the equation simplifies to

\begin{equation}
y[k] = \sum_{i=0}^{k} g[i].
\end{equation}

Depending on the system dynamics, the resulting step response does not necessarily converge to a final value of one. However, for a better interpretation of the results and an easier comparison between different controller settings, the step response is normalized using its steady-state mean value

\begin{equation}
y_{\mathrm{norm}}[k]
=
\frac{y[k]}
{\overline{y}},
\end{equation}

where \(\overline{y}\) denotes the mean value of the settled response. This scales the final value of the step response approximately to one.

Similar to the system step response, a step response can also be calculated for the compliance function \(SP(\omega)\). The calculation follows exactly the same procedure as described above.

While the system step response evaluates the tracking behaviour of the controller, the compliance response provides insight into how effectively external disturbances are rejected. This allows a direct comparison between the response to reference changes and the response to external disturbances.

The only difference is the final scaling. For the compliance response, the steady-state value is not normalized to one. Instead, the mean value within a selected steady-state interval is subtracted from the complete response

\begin{equation}
y_{\mathrm{comp,corr}}[k]
=
y_{\mathrm{comp}}[k]
-
\overline{y}_{\mathrm{comp}},
\end{equation}

where

\begin{equation}
\overline{y}_{\mathrm{comp}}
=
\mathrm{mean}
\left(
y_{\mathrm{comp}}[k_1:k_2]
\right).
\end{equation}

For controllers without integral action, a constant disturbance typically results in a steady-state offset. In contrast, integral action compensates constant disturbances over time and drives the response back to its original value. Subtracting the steady-state mean therefore highlights the transient disturbance rejection behaviour and facilitates the comparison of different controller settings.

\section{Theoretical Plant Models}

The goal of this thesis is to analyze the control loops of the Betaflight flight controller based on measurements obtained from flight experiments. To validate the proposed measurement and analysis methods, theoretical plant models are introduced as reference systems.

By comparing the experimentally identified system behaviour with these theoretical models, the accuracy and reliability of the applied analysis techniques can be assessed. The models also provide a framework for understanding the interaction between the different control loops implemented in Betaflight. The following subsections describe the plant models corresponding to the main control layers of the flight controller: angular rate, angle, altitude hold and position hold.

\subsection*{Motor Dynamics}

As the basis of the overall system, the transfer function of the motor dynamics must be determined. Since the internal implementation of the BLHeli32 ESC firmware is proprietary and not publicly available, the exact dynamics of the motor--ESC system are unknown. The following model therefore does not represent the real system exactly but serves as a simplified approximation of the dominant motor dynamics.

The motor--propeller system is modeled using the well-established electromechanical model of a DC motor, which can also be applied to brushless DC (BLDC) motors in an equivalent form \parencite{quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017}. The model consists of one electrical and one mechanical differential equation.

The electrical behaviour of the motor is described by
\begin{equation}
    u(t) = R\,i(t) + L\,\frac{di(t)}{dt} + k_e\,\omega(t),
    \label{eq:dc_motor_electrical}
\end{equation}
where $u(t)$ is the applied motor voltage, $i(t)$ the motor current, $R$ the winding resistance, $L$ the inductance, $k_e$ the back electromotive force constant and $\omega(t)$ the angular velocity.

The mechanical dynamics are given by
\begin{equation}
    J_{\mathrm{m}}\,\frac{d\omega(t)}{dt} = k_t\,i(t),
    \label{eq:dc_motor_mechanical}
\end{equation}
where $J_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the combined moment of inertia of the motor and propeller and $k_t$ is the torque constant. For simplicity, viscous friction and aerodynamic load torque are neglected.

Applying the Laplace transform to Eqs.~(\ref{eq:dc_motor_electrical}) and (\ref{eq:dc_motor_mechanical}) and eliminating $i(s)$ yields the transfer function from voltage to angular velocity:
\begin{equation}
    G_{\text{motor}}(s) = \frac{\Omega(s)}{U(s)} =
    \frac{k_t}{J_{\mathrm{m}} L s^2 + J_{\mathrm{m}} R s + k_t k_e}.
    \label{eq:dc_motor_transfer}
\end{equation}

Since the torque constant and the back electromotive force constant have the same numerical value, the transfer function can be simplified to
\begin{equation}
    G_{\text{motor}}(s) =
    \frac{k}{J_{\mathrm{m}} L s^2 + J_{\mathrm{m}} R s + k^2}.
\end{equation}

This transfer function represents a second-order (PT2) system, capturing both electrical and mechanical dynamics. Due to the small electrical time constant, the inductance mainly affects higher frequencies and can often be neglected. In this case, the motor can be approximated by a first-order (PT1) system, which forms the basis for the subsequent analysis and controller design.

\subsection*{Plant Angular Rate}

The angular rate controller forms the innermost control loop of the flight controller and directly influences the performance of the outer loops, such as the angle and position controllers. Therefore, its plant model serves as the basis for the overall system analysis.

Based on the motor dynamics described in the previous section, the plant of the angular rate controller can be derived. The input to this plant is the applied motor voltage, while the output is the resulting angular rate. The transfer function between the input voltage and the motor speed is given by the previously introduced motor model. However, the motor dynamics alone do not fully describe the behaviour of the drone. The relationship between motor speed and angular rate is additionally influenced by the drone geometry and the aerodynamic forces generated by the propellers.

A change in motor speed results in a change in propeller thrust. Due to the distance between the motors and the centre of gravity of the drone, these thrust differences generate torques about the rotational axes. The resulting angular acceleration is proportional to the applied torque and inversely proportional to the moment of inertia of the drone. Integrating the angular acceleration yields the angular rate, which is the controlled variable of the angular rate controller.

The thrust produced by each propeller can be approximated by

\begin{equation}
    T = C_T \rho n^2 D^4,
\end{equation}

where $C_T$ is the thrust coefficient, $\rho$ the air density, $n$ the rotational speed and $D$ the propeller diameter \parencite{quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017}.

Differential thrust between opposing motors generates a torque that causes the drone to rotate about its axes. The resulting torque is proportional to the thrust difference and the distance from the center of mass to the motor, denoted by $l$ and can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
    M_{\text{roll}} = l \cdot \Delta T.
\end{equation}

This expression shows that the generated torque is directly proportional to both the thrust difference and the lever arm, highlighting the importance of the drone geometry for the rotational dynamics.

\begin{equation}
    \alpha_{\text{roll}} = \frac{M_{\text{roll}}}{J_{\text{roll}}},
\end{equation}

where $J_{\text{roll}}$ is the moment of inertia about the roll axis. This relation highlights that the rotational dynamics depend both on the generated thrust difference and on the mass distribution of the drone.

Integrating the angular acceleration yields the angular rate. The resulting transfer function is

\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Angular Rate}}(s) = \frac{\Omega(s)}{U(s)} =
    \frac{k_t}{J_{\mathrm{m}} L s^2 + J_{\mathrm{m}} R s + k_t k_e}
    \cdot
    \frac{l}{J_{\text{roll}}\,s}.
\end{equation}

This transfer function exhibits an integrating behaviour at low frequencies, resulting in a slope of approximately \(-20\,\text{dB/dec}\). At higher frequencies, the motor dynamics introduce additional phase lag and a steeper magnitude roll-off, reflecting the limited bandwidth of the actuator system.

To validate the theoretical model, the frequency response of the plant is estimated from flight data using the previously described identification method. The resulting Bode plot and coherence are shown in \autoref{fig:plant_gyro_measured}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{Plant_Gyro.png}
    \caption{Estimated frequency response of the angular rate plant (roll axis)}
    \label{fig:plant_gyro_measured}
\end{figure}

The estimated response shows good agreement with the expected behaviour in the low- to mid-frequency range, exhibiting the characteristic \(-20\,\text{dB/dec}\) slope. This confirms the integrator behaviour of the plant.

At higher frequencies, deviations occur due to motor dynamics and unmodeled effects such as aerodynamic disturbances. The coherence remains high over a wide frequency range and decreases at higher frequencies due to measurement noise and reduced signal-to-noise ratio.
\parencite{lutzTaschenbuchRegelungstechnik}

\subsection*{Plant Angle}

The angle plant is considerably simpler than the angular rate plant. It is based on the fundamental kinematic relationship between the angular rate and the orientation of the multirotor. The angle corresponds to the time integral of the angular rate, which can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \theta(t) = \int \omega(t)\, dt.
\end{equation}

Assuming zero initial conditions and applying the Laplace transform, this relationship becomes
\begin{equation}
    \Theta(s) = \frac{1}{s}\,\Omega(s).
\end{equation}

This shows that the angle is obtained by integrating the angular rate, corresponding to a multiplication by $\frac{1}{s}$ in the Laplace domain. Consequently, from a theoretical point of view, the angle plant behaves as an ideal integrator with the transfer function
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Angle}}(s) = \frac{\theta(s)}{\omega(s)} = \frac{1}{s}.
\end{equation}

Through these calculations, we know that we can expect a Bode plot of the angle plant with a slope of -20 dB/decade and a phase shift of -90 degrees, which are characteristic features of an ideal integrator \parencite{quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017}.

To verify this theoretical behaviour, the frequency response of the angle plant is estimated from flight data using the system identification approach described earlier. The resulting Bode plot and coherence are shown in \autoref{fig:plant_angle_measured}.

    \caption{Estimated frequency response of the angle plant (roll axis)}
    \label{fig:plant_angle_measured}
\end{figure}

The measured frequency response confirms the expected integrator behaviour at low frequencies. In particular, the magnitude exhibits an approximate -20 dB/dec slope and the phase remains close to -90°, as predicted by the theoretical model.

At higher frequencies, deviations from the ideal integrator behaviour can be observed. These effects are mainly caused by measurement noise, limited excitation and unmodeled dynamics in the system.

The coherence is close to one in the low-frequency range, indicating a reliable estimate. As the frequency increases, the coherence decreases, suggesting that the estimation becomes less reliable due to noise and reduced signal-to-noise ratio.

\subsection*{Plant Position Hold}

The position hold controller includes the angular rate and the angle as inner control loops. Therefore, the plant of the position hold corresponds to the transformation from the drone's attitude (angle) to its position in space. This transformation is determined by the kinematics of the multirotor and the influence of external forces such as gravity.

The position of the drone can be described by its coordinates in three-dimensional space, typically denoted as \( (x, y, z) \). The orientation of the drone influences how the thrust generated by the motors is directed and thus determines its motion in space.

If the drone is hovering at a constant altitude, the total thrust equals the weight of the drone, given by \( mg \), where \( m \) is the mass of the drone and \( g \) is the gravitational acceleration. When the drone tilts by a small angle \( \theta \), a horizontal component of the thrust is generated, leading to a horizontal acceleration, as illustrated in \autoref{fig:drone_pos_hold}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{Drone_Pos_Hold.png}
    \caption{Illustration of a quadcopter tilted by an angle \( \theta \) during position hold. The tilt redirects the thrust vector, generating a horizontal acceleration that results in translational motion.}
    \label{fig:drone_pos_hold}
\end{figure}

The horizontal acceleration can be expressed as
\begin{equation}
    a_{\text{hor}}(t) = g \cdot \sin\!\left(\theta(t)\right).
\end{equation}

Assuming that the drone operates at small angles during position hold, the small-angle approximation \( \sin(\theta) \approx \theta \) can be applied \parencite{quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017}. This results in the simplified expression
\begin{equation}
    a_{\text{hor}}(t) \approx g \cdot \theta(t).
\end{equation}

By integrating the horizontal acceleration twice with respect to time, the horizontal position of the drone is obtained:
\begin{equation}
    x(t) = \int \int a_{\text{hor}}(t) \, dt^2
    \approx \int \int g \cdot \theta(t) \, dt^2.
\end{equation}

Applying the Laplace transform yields the transfer function from the tilt angle to the horizontal position:
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Position}}(s) = \frac{X(s)}{\Theta(s)} = \frac{g}{s^2}.
\end{equation}

The measured frequency response generally follows the expected double-integrator characteristic. However, a deviation can be observed around \(0.6\,\mathrm{Hz}\). One possible explanation is the increasing violation of the small-angle assumption used in the derivation of the plant model. As the excitation frequency increases, larger attitude angles are required to generate the corresponding lateral acceleration. Consequently, the approximation \(\sin(\theta)\approx\theta\) becomes less accurate and the real system behaviour deviates from the idealized double-integrator model.

        \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{Plant_PosHold.png}
    }
    \caption{Estimated frequency response of the Position Hold plant for the roll (black) and pitch (red) axes.}
    \label{fig:plant_pos_hold}
\end{figure}

\subsection*{Plant Altitude Hold}

The altitude hold directly controls the vertical position of the drone by adjusting the total thrust generated by the motors. Consequently, the plant describes the transformation from motor speed to vertical position.

The altitude plant consists of the motor dynamics and the transformation from motor speed to vertical motion.

The thrust produced by a propeller can be approximated by

\begin{equation}
    T = C_T \rho n^2 D^4,
\end{equation}

where \(C_T\) is the thrust coefficient, \(\rho\) the air density, \(n\) the rotational speed and \(D\) the propeller diameter \parencite{quanIntroductionMulticopterDesign2017}.

The vertical acceleration follows from the force balance

\begin{equation}
    a_{\text{ver}}(t) = \frac{T(t)}{m} - g.
\end{equation}

For hover, the thrust balances the weight of the drone:

\begin{equation}
    T_0 = m g .
\end{equation}

The corresponding hover speed is
\begin{equation}
    n_0 = \sqrt{\frac{T_0}{C_T \rho D^4}} .
\end{equation}

For small deviations \( \Delta n \) around the operating point \( n_0 \), linearizing the thrust equation yields

\begin{equation}
    \Delta T \approx 
    \left.\frac{dT}{dn}\right|_{n_0} \Delta n,
    \quad \text{with} \quad
    \frac{dT}{dn} = 2 C_T \rho D^4 n.
\end{equation}

Substituting \(n_0\) gives
\begin{equation}
    \Delta T \approx 
    2 C_T \rho D^4
    \sqrt{\frac{T_0}{C_T \rho D^4}}
    \,\Delta n .
\end{equation}

The corresponding vertical acceleration becomes
\begin{equation}
    \Delta a_{\text{ver}}(t) = \frac{\Delta T(t)}{m}.
\end{equation}

Applying the Laplace transform and integrating twice yields
\begin{equation}
    \Delta S(s) = \frac{1}{s^2}\Delta A_{\text{ver}}(s).
\end{equation}

Thus, the transfer function from motor speed variation to vertical position is
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Delta S(s)}{\Delta N(s)} = \frac{2 C_T \rho D^4}{m s^2}
    \sqrt{\frac{T_0}{C_T \rho D^4}}.
\end{equation}

Including the motor dynamics, the complete altitude plant becomes
\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Altitude}}(s) = \frac{\Delta S(s)}{\Delta U(s)}
    = \frac{2 C_T \rho D^4}{m s^2}\sqrt{\frac{T_0}{C_T \rho D^4}}\,P_{\text{Motor}}(s).
\end{equation}

The plant exhibits a double integrator behaviour, resulting in a slope of \(-40\,\text{dB/decade}\) at low frequencies, while the motor dynamics introduce additional phase lag at higher frequencies.

To validate the derived model, the frequency response of the altitude plant is estimated from flight data using the previously described identification method. The resulting Bode plot and coherence are shown in \autoref{fig:plant_altitude_measured}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{Plant_AltHold.png}
    \caption{Estimated frequency response of the altitude plant. Magnitude, phase and coherence are shown.}
    \label{fig:plant_altitude_measured}
\end{figure}

The estimated frequency response confirms the expected behaviour at low frequencies, where the magnitude follows approximately a \(-40\,\text{dB/dec}\) slope, consistent with the double integrator model.

\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}

In this chapter, the practical implementation of the previously introduced theoretical concepts is presented. The focus lies on the application of the proposed system identification and offline tuning methods to the different control loops of a Betaflight-based flight controller.

For each control loop, the same general workflow is applied. A chirp signal is injected into the control loop, the resulting flight data is recorded and the frequency response is estimated. Based on these measurements, the corresponding plant and controller dynamics are determined and used for the tuning process.

The methodology is structured according to the individual control loops. Starting with the angular rate controller, the corresponding control structures, firmware changes, transfer function estimations and tuning procedures are described. The same approach is then extended to the angle, altitude hold and position hold controllers.

Additionally, the required firmware modifications, measurement procedures and practical implementation challenges are discussed. The chapter also highlights limitations and observations encountered during the experimental evaluation of the different control loops.

\section{Gyro Controller}

The gyro controller is the innermost control loop of the flight controller and directly regulates the angular velocity of the drone. Due to its position within the control hierarchy, it operates at a high update rate and reacts quickly to disturbances, making it one of the most important control loops for the overall flight behaviour and stability of the drone.

The offline tuning framework for the gyro controller was originally developed during the preceding project thesis and serves as the foundation for the methods presented in this work. Therefore, only the most relevant implementation details and results are summarized in this chapter, while the focus lies on the practical application and extension of the tuning methodology within the overall framework.

\subsection*{Betaflight Firmware}

During a recent Betaflight release, a new chirp feature, developed by Michael Peter, was added to the firmware. This feature allows a chirp signal to be injected directly into the control loop during flight. It forms the basis for the offline tuning method used in this work.

The main idea of this approach is to excite the control loop over a wide frequency range and use the recorded flight data to estimate the transfer functions of the different control loop components. Compared to normal flight manoeuvres, the chirp signal provides a more controlled and systematic excitation of the system, which improves the quality of the estimated frequency responses and the controller analysis.

Further details about the chirp signal and the underlying theory are described in the theoretical part of this thesis.

\subsubsection*{Acro Mode Control Loop}

In Acro mode, the control loop is designed to directly regulate the angular rates of the drone. The reference input to the controller is the desired angular rate, which is derived from the pilot's stick inputs. The controller generates the required motor commands to achieve the desired angular rates. The chirp signal is added to the reference input, allowing the excitation of the system over a wide frequency range. This enables the estimation of the transfer functions of the gyro control loop components.

The control loop in Acro mode can be represented as follows:

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Regelkreis_Gyro.png}
    \caption{Control loop structure in Acro mode showing the chirp signal injection, the PI and D controller branches, the real drone dynamics (\textit{P Gyro}), the gyro filter and the disturbance inputs used for system identification and frequency-response analysis.}
    \label{fig:control_loop_acro}
\end{figure}

\autoref{fig:control_loop_acro} shows the complete gyro control loop in Acro mode. The reference input $w$ (desired angular rate) is combined with a chirp signal to excite the system over a wide frequency range. The resulting signal is fed into a PI controller, which generates the main control action.

After the controller, input disturbances are added before the signal enters the plant $P_{\text{Gyro}}$, representing the real drone dynamics. Output disturbances are introduced after the plant and before the gyro filtering stage. The measured angular rate $y$ is then fed back to the controller.

A key feature of this control structure is the placement of the D-controller in the feedback path instead of the forward path. The D-controller acts on the measured output signal and is fed back negatively. This configuration is used to compensate for a zero in the plant dynamics, improving the system stability while reducing the noise amplification that would occur if the derivative term were applied directly to the reference signal.

Additionally, dedicated filters are used to attenuate high-frequency noise before it affects the control action. This is especially important for the D-controller, since the derivative term is generally very sensitive to noise. Without the D-path filter, measurement noise would strongly affect the D-term and therefore the controller output, which is not desired and could lead to unstable behaviour.

\newpage

\subsection*{Control Loop Calculation}

Based on the control loop structure in Acro mode, the different control loop components can be estimated from the measured input and output signals. Using the recorded chirp measurements together with block diagram algebra, the plant, controller and overall closed-loop behaviour can be separated and analysed individually.

As described in the theoretical part of this thesis, all transfer functions are estimated with respect to the same reference input signal. This simplifies the signal processing and improves the quality of the estimated frequency responses, since the influence of the closed-loop feedback is reduced. The estimated transfer functions then form the basis for the offline tuning process and allow the dynamic behaviour of the different control loop components to be analysed separately.

\subsubsection*{Estimation of the Closed-Loop Components}

By estimating different transfer functions from the measured data, the individual components of the control loop can be analysed separately. This allows the controller, plant and feedback paths to be evaluated individually instead of only observing the overall closed-loop response. As described in the theoretical part of this thesis, this approach improves the system analysis and the quality of the estimated transfer functions.

The first step is to estimate the closed-loop transfer function from the reference input \( w \) to the measured output \( y \), which is denoted as \( G_{yw} \):

\begin{equation}
T_{\text{Gyro,meas}}(\omega)=G_{\text{yw}}(\omega) = \frac{Y(\omega)}{W(\omega)}
\end{equation}

where \( Y(\omega) \) is the Fourier transform of the measured output signal and \( W(\omega) \) is the Fourier transform of the reference input, including the chirp signal. These signals are filtered and processed as described in the theory chapter in order to reduce noise and improve the quality of the estimated frequency response.

The transfer function \( G_{\text{yw}} \) represents the overall closed-loop behaviour of the system and can be used to evaluate important characteristics such as bandwidth, phase behaviour and stability margins.

To estimate the plant transfer function, the transfer function from the reference input to the controller output \( u \) is additionally calculated:

\begin{equation}
G_{\text{uw}}(\omega) = \frac{U(\omega)}{W(\omega)}
\end{equation}

where \( U(\omega) \) is the Fourier transform of the controller output signal. This transfer function describes the dynamic relationship between the reference input and the control action applied to the plant.

To estimate the D-term of the controller, the transfer function from the reference input to the signal after the PI controller and the D-path filter is additionally required. This transfer function is denoted as \( G_{\text{vw}} \):

\begin{equation}
G_{\text{vw}}(\omega) = \frac{V(\omega)}{W(\omega)}
\end{equation}

where \( V(\omega) \) is the Fourier transform of the signal after the PI controller and the D-path filter. This additional transfer function is required because the D-term is implemented in the feedback path of the control loop and therefore cannot directly be separated from the remaining controller dynamics.

With these transfer functions, the plant transfer function can be estimated as

\begin{equation}
G_{\text{yv}}(\omega) = \frac{T_{\text{Gyro,meas}}(\omega)}{G_{\text{uw}}(\omega)}
\end{equation}

In this estimation, the influence of the gyro filter is still included in the estimated plant dynamics. Therefore, the estimated plant transfer function is divided by the gyro filter transfer function to obtain the real plant dynamics:

\begin{equation}
P_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega) = \frac{G_{\text{yv}}(\omega)}{G_{\text{FilterGyro}}(\omega)}
\end{equation}

The transfer function of the PI controller can be estimated as

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{PI,meas}}(\omega) = \frac{G_{\text{uw}}(\omega)}{1 - T_{\text{Gyro,meas}}(\omega)}
\end{equation}

This estimation is required for the compensation of the I-Term Relax algorithm described in the theory chapter. By comparing the estimated transfer function with the analytical PI controller model, a compensation factor can be calculated and applied to both the original and the newly designed controller.

The D-term and its filter can be estimated in a similar way:

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{D,meas}}(\omega) = \frac{G_{\text{uw}}(\omega) - G_{\text{vw}}(\omega)}{T_{\text{Gyro,meas}}(\omega)}
\end{equation}

The estimation of the D-term was especially useful during the development of the tuning framework, since it helped to better understand the D-term behaviour and identify implementation issues within the controller structure.

\newpage

\subsubsection*{Calculation of the Tuning Responses}

Based on the estimated plant transfer function obtained from the flight measurements, the influence of different PID and filter parameters on the control loop behaviour can be analysed offline. By combining the measured plant dynamics with analytical controller models, new closed-loop transfer functions can be calculated without performing additional flight tests.

As described in the theoretical part of this thesis, the Gang of Four transfer functions can be used to analyze the behaviour and robustness of the control loop for different controller and filter parameters.

The relevant components for the angular rate controller are the PI controller \(C_{\text{PI}}\), the D-controller \(C_d\), the gyro filter \(G_{\text{FilterGyro}}\), which can be adjusted during tuning and the estimated plant \(P_{\text{Gyro}}\), which was obtained from the measured data.

With these components, the transfer functions for the Gang of Four analysis can be calculated. First, the closed-loop transfer function of the angular rate control loop is given by

\begin{equation}
T_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)
=
\frac{
C_{\text{PI}}(\omega)\,
P_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)\, 
G_{\text{FilterGyro}}(\omega)
}{
1 +
P_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)
\left(
C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_{\text{d}}(\omega)
\right)
G_{\text{FilterGyro}}(\omega)
}
\end{equation}

The sensitivity function can also be calculated with the new controller as follows. As already discussed in the theoretical part, the sensitivity function is important for evaluating the robustness of the system and the disturbance rejection behaviour:

\begin{equation}
S_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)
=
\frac{
1
}{
1 +
P_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)
\left(
C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_{\text{d}}(\omega)
\right)
G_{\text{FilterGyro}}(\omega)
}
\end{equation}

The compliance function shows how disturbances acting on the plant affect the system output:

\begin{equation}
SP_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)
=
\frac{
P_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)
}{
1 +
P_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)
\left(
C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_{\text{d}}(\omega)
\right)
G_{\text{FilterGyro}}(\omega)
}
\end{equation}

The controller effort can also be calculated with the new controller as follows. This function can be used to evaluate the controller activity and the amplification of noise at higher frequencies:

\begin{equation}
SC_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)
=
\frac{
\left(
C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_{\text{d}}(\omega)
\right)
G_{\text{FilterGyro}}(\omega)
}{
1 +
P_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)
\left(
C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_{\text{d}}(\omega)
\right)
G_{\text{FilterGyro}}(\omega)
}
\end{equation}

By analyzing these transfer functions, the influence of different PID and filter parameters on the control loop behaviour can be evaluated. This allows the controller tuning to be analysed offline before performing additional flight tests.

\section{Angle Controller}

The Betaflight software provides three main flight modes: Angle, Horizon and Acro.

In Angle mode, the drone’s tilt angle is directly linked to the stick position. The maximum tilt is limited to a predefined value, preventing flips and making this mode particularly suitable for beginners.

In contrast, Acro mode provides full manual control. In this mode, stick inputs define an angular rate rather than a target angle, meaning that the drone continues rotating as long as the stick remains deflected. This allows aggressive maneuvers such as flips and rolls but also requires greater pilot experience.

An offline tuning method for the angular rate controller was already available from previous project work and serves as the basis for the present implementation. Since the angle controller is built on top of the angular rate controller, the existing approach can be extended to Angle mode with only minor modifications. This enables the systematic tuning of the angle control loop while preserving the intuitive handling characteristics of Angle mode.


This extension allows a consistent tuning methodology across different flight modes, improving overall system performance and usability.

\subsection*{Betaflight Firmware}
This chapter describes the implementation of the Betaflight firmware and the modifications required for the proposed offline tuning approach.

The main focus lies on the Angle mode controller. Compared to the standard Acro mode, Angle mode adds an additional outer control loop for attitude stabilization. This results in a cascaded control structure and changes the overall system dynamics compared to the normal rate-controlled flight mode.

In the standard Betaflight implementation, many internal controller signals are not directly accessible through the logging system. However, these signals are required for the offline analysis and transfer function estimation used in this work. Therefore, small modifications were made to the firmware to expose relevant internal controller variables through the existing debug infrastructure.

Additionally, the chirp signal injection used for system excitation had to be adapted for the Angle mode controller. Depending on the injection point of the chirp signal, different parts of the control loop can be analysed. The implemented modifications therefore also allow the chirp signal to be injected at different positions in the control structure depending on the active flight mode.

The modifications were intentionally kept as small as possible in order to avoid influencing the original flight behaviour and the real-time performance of the firmware. The goal was not to redesign the existing controller implementation, but rather to extend the firmware with the functionality required for offline analysis and controller tuning.

\subsubsection*{Angle Mode Control Loop}

In Angle mode, the angular rate controller is extended by an additional outer angle control loop, resulting in a cascaded control structure. Unlike Acro mode, the pilot stick input no longer directly defines an angular rate, but instead a target angle for the drone orientation.

The outer angle loop operates at a slower dynamic compared to the inner rate loop. Its task is to generate stable angular rate setpoints that move the drone toward the desired orientation. The inner angular rate controller then reacts much faster and stabilizes the rotational motion of the quadcopter.

The cascaded architecture separates attitude control from angular rate stabilization. While the outer loop ensures convergence toward the desired orientation, the inner loop provides fast dynamic stabilization by regulating the angular velocity. As a result, the system achieves improved disturbance rejection and more intuitive handling characteristics, since the pilot directly commands an angle rather than a rotational rate.

This behavior makes Angle mode especially suitable for beginner pilots and for applications where stable and predictable flight behavior is required. In contrast to Acro mode, the drone automatically returns to the commanded orientation when the stick input is centered.

The Angle mode controller therefore consists of two cascaded loops:
\begin{itemize}
\item The outer angle loop, which computes the angle error.
\item The inner angular rate loop, which computes the rate error.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Figures/Control_Loop_Angle.png}
\caption{Cascaded control structure of the Betaflight Angle mode controller.}
\label{fig:angle_mode_control_loop}
\end{figure}

The angle control loop consists of a proportional controller and an additional feedforward term. While the feedforward improves responsiveness during normal flight operation by directly reacting to stick inputs, it complicates offline system identification because part of the controller output no longer originates from the feedback loop.

To simplify transfer function estimation and controller tuning, the feedforward term is disabled in the modified firmware. This allows the angle control loop to be analyzed as a purely proportional controller.

\newpage

\subsubsection*{Derivation of the Target Angle}

In the control structure shown in \autoref{fig:angle_mode_control_loop}, the block ``Transformation into Degree'' converts the commanded angular rate into a target angle for the outer angle control loop. The target angle is obtained by scaling the commanded angular rate with respect to the configured maximum angular rate and the maximum allowed tilt angle:

\begin{equation}
\theta_{\text{target}} =
\theta_{\text{limit}}
\cdot
\frac{\omega_{\text{set}}}{\omega_{\text{max}}}
\label{eq:target_angle}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\theta_{\text{limit}}$ is the maximum allowed tilt angle,
    \item $\omega_{\text{set}}$ is the commanded angular rate derived from the stick input,
    \item $\omega_{\text{max}}$ is the maximum configured angular rate.
\end{itemize}

This proportional mapping allows the pilot to control the drone orientation directly through the stick position while limiting the maximum achievable tilt angle.

\subsubsection*{Firmware Modifications}

\begingroup
\emergencystretch=1em

The chirp generator described in the gyro-controller tuning section is reused here. Its public interface (\texttt{chirpInit}, \texttt{chirpUpdate} in \texttt{src/\allowbreak main/\allowbreak common/\allowbreak chirp.c}) and the invocation of \texttt{chirpInit} inside \texttt{pidInit} (\texttt{src/\allowbreak main/\allowbreak flight/\allowbreak pid\_\allowbreak init.c}) are unchanged; the configured frequency range, sweep time and per-axis amplitudes are reused from the gyro-tuning configuration. Injecting the chirp at the same point as in the gyro case would, however, leave the excitation inside the closed angle loop: the inner-loop rate setpoint would be driven simultaneously by the angle controller's output and by the chirp itself, so the transfer function from the angle target to the measured angle could not be recovered from the resulting logs directly. The relevant existing structure is shown in \autoref{fig:angle_mode_control_loop}.

To make this transfer function identifiable, a small modification was made to \texttt{pidController} (\texttt{src/\allowbreak main/\allowbreak flight/\allowbreak pid.c}) so that, while \texttt{CHIRP\_\allowbreak MODE} is engaged and the affected axis is in angle mode, the chirp value is injected upstream of \texttt{pidLevel}, before the rate-to-angle transformation, rather than at the inner-loop rate setpoint. \texttt{pidLevel} first applies a linear transformation from the (rate-domain) \texttt{currentPidSetpoint} to a target angle, scaled by the configured tilt-angle limit divided by the maximum commanded rate. Because this transformation is linear, the \texttt{chirp\_\allowbreak amplitude\_\allowbreak *} values calibrated for rate-loop excitation map proportionally onto the angle-target excitation and carry over without re-tuning. The resulting structure is shown in \autoref{fig:angle_loop_modified}.

Inside \texttt{pidLevel}, the rate-domain setpoint is converted into a target angle using a linear transformation based on the configured maximum tilt angle and commanded rate. Because this transformation is linear, the chirp amplitudes used for gyro tuning can be reused without additional adjustments. The resulting control structure is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:angle_loop_modified}.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/Control_Loop_Angle_new.png}

The excitation signal is selected depending on the corresponding \texttt{levelMode} state. In acro mode, the existing gyro-tuning behaviour is retained, including the low-frequency amplitude taper below 1\,Hz. This prevents excessive angle excursions caused by the integrating behaviour of the rate loop.

To improve the signal-to-noise ratio, a new parameter called \texttt{chirp\_repeat} was added. This allows the same chirp sweep to be repeated multiple times before the sequencer advances to the next axis. A settling time of 750\,ms is inserted between repetitions. The parameter is limited to a maximum value of 9 so that the repeat counter can still be displayed in the OSD.

A lag filter is applied to the chirp signal before injection. The filter was already available in the upstream implementation and is configured through the \texttt{chirp\_lag\_freq\_hz} and \texttt{chirp\_lead\_freq\_hz} parameters. To avoid transients from previous measurements, an additional \texttt{phaseCompReset} function was introduced. The filter state is reset whenever \texttt{CHIRP\_MODE} is activated, a new axis is selected or a new repetition starts.

The internal signals required for the offline tuning tool are recorded through the existing \texttt{DEBUG\_CHIRP} channels. Besides the chirp phase, active axis, instantaneous frequency and excitation signal, the target and measured angle are also logged. These values are stored by the existing Blackbox system when \texttt{debug\_mode} is set to \texttt{CHIRP}, requiring no modifications to the Blackbox implementation itself.

All functionality described in this section is only included when the firmware is compiled with \texttt{USE\_CHIRP}. Without this option, the firmware remains functionally equivalent to the upstream baseline.

\subsection*{Control Loop Calculation}

Based on the modified control structure, the dynamic behavior of the Angle mode control loop can be analyzed using the recorded input and output signals. By injecting the chirp signal before the transformation to the target angle, the system can be treated as a single-input single-output (SISO) system from the target angle to the measured angle.

This allows the same signal processing steps as described in the theory chapter to be applied, including noise filtering and windowing. Based on the measured frequency responses, the transfer functions of the cascaded control loop components can then be estimated directly from the recorded flight data.

\subsubsection*{Estimation of the Closed-Loop Components}

Using the Fourier transforms of the target angle reference signal \(w\) and the measured angle \(q\), shown in \autoref{fig:angle_loop_modified}, the closed-loop transfer function can be estimated as

\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{Angle,meas}} = G_{\text{cl}}(\omega) =
    \frac{Q(\omega)}{W(\omega)}.
    \label{eq:G_cl}
\end{equation}

This transfer function describes the complete behavior of the cascaded control structure, including both the outer angle controller and the inner angular rate controller, as well as the plant dynamics.

As described in the theoretical part of this thesis, it is also necessary to estimate the transfer function from the target angle \(w\) to the angular rate \(y\):

\begin{equation}
    G_{\text{wy}}(\omega) =
    \frac{Y(\omega)}{W(\omega)}.
    \label{eq:G_wy}
\end{equation}

With the two transfer functions \(T_{Angle,meas}(\omega)\) and \(G_{wy}(\omega)\), the plant of the angle control loop can be calculated as follows:

\begin{equation}
    P_{\text{Angle}}(\omega) =
    \frac{T_{\text{Angle,meas}}(\omega)}{G_{\text{wy}}(\omega)}.
    \label{eq:G_plant}
\end{equation}

The resulting plant transfer function does not only contain the physical drone dynamics, but also the complete closed-loop behavior of the inner angular rate controller. From the perspective of the outer angle loop, the stabilized rate controller therefore becomes part of the plant dynamics.

The estimated plant transfer function can then be used to analyze the influence of different controller parameters on the Angle mode behavior without performing additional flight tests.

\subsubsection*{Implementation of the Tuning Method}

The calculation of the tuning parameters for the angle control loop follows the same approach as for the angular rate control loop, but uses different control loop components. The relevant components are the gyro control loop \(T_{\text{Gyro}}\), which is obtained from the angular rate control loop calculations, the angle controller and filter combined into \(C_{\text{Angle}}\), and the plant \(P_{\text{Angle}}\).

With these components, the transfer functions for the Gang of Four plot can be calculated. First, the closed-loop transfer function of the angle control loop is

\begin{equation}
T_{\text{Angle}}(\omega) =
\frac{C_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)\,T_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)}
{1 + C_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)\,T_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)}.
\label{eq:G_cl_new}
\end{equation}

Furthermore, the sensitivity function, which characterizes the disturbance rejection and robustness of the control system, can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
S_{\text{Angle}}(\omega) =
\frac{1}{1 + C_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)\,T_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)}.
\label{eq:S_new}
\end{equation}

The controller effort can also be calculated. It describes how strongly the controller output reacts to the reference input and is given by

\begin{equation}
SC_{\text{Angle}}(\omega) =
\frac{C_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)}
{1 + C_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)\,T_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)}.
\label{eq:G_u_new}
\end{equation}

Additionally, the compliance transfer function can be calculated. This transfer function describes how disturbances acting on the plant influence the system output:

\begin{equation}
SP_{\text{Angle}}(\omega) =
\frac{P_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)}
{1 + C_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)\,T_{\text{Gyro}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{Angle}}(\omega)}.
\label{eq:G_c_new}
\end{equation}

These expressions allow the computation of all relevant performance metrics such as gain margin, phase margin, bandwidth, controller effort, disturbance attenuation and robustness properties. Based on these metrics, plots can be generated and the suitability of the new controller parameters can be systematically evaluated.

\section{Altitude Hold Controller}

The altitude hold control loop maintains a constant altitude by adjusting the throttle based on the measured vertical position. The altitude estimation is mainly based on GPS measurements fused with IMU sensor data to estimate the current vertical position and motion of the drone.

During the development of the tuning tool, major changes were made to the Betaflight firmware affecting the altitude hold functionality and control loop. Therefore, only a proof of concept based on the firmware version from March 2025 was implemented. As a result, the modified firmware will not be merged into the Betaflight codebase and the tuning tool will not be implemented in Python for community use.

This chapter presents the firmware modifications required for applying the tuning method to the altitude hold controller. It also describes the control loop calculation and the tuning process, including the evaluation of the step response and the Gang of Four. Furthermore, the chapter is intended to support future developers once the ongoing firmware changes are completed and the altitude hold controller has stabilized.


\subsection*{Altitude Hold Control Loop}
In the altitude hold mode, an additional control loop gets activated. It controls the thrust part of the motors as described in the motor mixing chapter. Therefore, the drone is able to fly at a constant altitude without the pilot having to adjust the throttle stick. The altitude hold control loop is not cascaded as you can see in the \autoref{fig:altitude_hold_control_loop}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{control_loop_althold.png}
    \caption{Control structure of the Betaflight altitude hold controller.}
    \label{fig:altitude_hold_control_loop}
\end{figure}


\autoref{fig:altitude_hold_control_loop} shows the complete altitude control loop in the altitude hold mode. The reference input w which mostly is zero because the drone set the selected altitude to zero. The resulting signal is fed into a PI controller, which generates the main control action.

The output of the PI controller is then added with a feedback signal in which the D-Term is within. This feedback signal is there to compensate the I-term, which stops the drone form overshooting the target altitude. The resulting signal is then fed into the plant, which consists of the drone dynamics, the motors and the tilt multiplier. The tilt multiplier is a factor that scales the thrust based on the current tilt angle of the drone. This is necessary because the vertical component of the thrust decreases as the drone tilts and the control loop needs to compensate for this effect to maintain a stable altitude.

In the feedback path which includes the D-term, a D-Boost is implemented. During normal altitude hold operation, a rather small D-term is preferred in order to avoid unnecessary noise amplification and throttle oscillations while maintaining a constant altitude. During fast vertical movements of the drone, however, the D-Boost increases the D-term gain dynamically. This helps to improve the damping and transient behaviour of the altitude hold control loop, especially during rapid altitude changes. The D-Boost is calculated based on the estimated vertical velocity. For the measurements performed in this work, the D-Boost was disabled in order to obtain a system behaviour which is as linear as possible for the frequency-domain system identification.



\subsection*{Firmware Modifications}

\begingroup
\emergencystretch=1em

The Betaflight fork used in this work is based on the upstream master branch from February 2026 (commit \texttt{04e37a122}). All modifications are protected by the compile-time flag \texttt{USE\_ALTHOLD\_CHIRP}, ensuring that a build without this flag behaves identically to the upstream version. The implementation consists of a dedicated flight mode, an extension of the chirp generator, modifications to the altitude controller, additional debug outputs and several user-configurable parameters.

A new flight mode called \texttt{ALTHOLD\_CHIRP} was introduced to enable chirp-based system identification in altitude hold mode. The corresponding mode was added in \texttt{rc\_modes.c}, \texttt{rc\_modes.h} and \texttt{runtime\_config.h}. The mode can be activated via an RC switch and is only enabled when altitude hold is active. For safety reasons, the excitation is automatically disabled during failsafe or GPS rescue.

The chirp signal generation was implemented in \texttt{common/chirp.c}. While the original Betaflight chirp generator was designed for angular rate excitation, altitude hold requires a position-based excitation signal. Therefore, a new function called \texttt{altChirpUpdate} was added, which reuses the existing logarithmic frequency sweep but generates a direct sine signal suitable for altitude control.

The chirp is injected into the altitude setpoint within \texttt{altHoldUpdate} in \texttt{alt\_hold\_multirotor.c}. When the mode is activated, the current target altitude is stored as a reference value. The chirp signal is then added to this reference, causing the altitude setpoint to oscillate symmetrically around the hover point. When the mode is disabled, the generator is reset to ensure reproducible measurements.

To improve the quality of the frequency-response estimation, two nonlinear controller elements were temporarily disabled during the chirp excitation in \texttt{altitudeControl} within \texttt{autopilot\_multirotor.c}. These are the I-term-relax scaling and the velocity-dependent D-boost term. Disabling both features helps to maintain approximately linear controller behaviour during the measurement and improves the accuracy of the identified transfer function.

The recorded signals include the chirp input, altitude, altitude setpoint, PID controller outputs, vertical velocity and throttle command. These signals are logged through the existing Blackbox infrastructure and form the basis for the frequency-domain identification process.

Additional feedback was provided to the pilot through the on-screen display. A dedicated flight-mode indicator shows when the chirp mode is active, while a warning message informs the user when the frequency sweep has been completed.

Finally, four new CLI parameters were added to configure the chirp amplitude, start frequency, end frequency and sweep duration. These parameters allow the excitation signal to be adapted to different vehicles and measurement scenarios without modifying the source code.

\subsection*{Control Loop Calculation}

With the injected chirp signal and the recorded data, the control loop can be calculated. This happens through the same steps as described in the theory chapter, including noise filtering and windowing. The resulting transfer function describes the complete behaviour of the altitude hold control loop, including the plant dynamics and the controller. Through some algebraic manipulations, the plant and the controller can be separated, which allows for a systematic tuning of the controller parameters based on the estimated plant dynamics.

By analyzing the estimated transfer functions in the frequency domain, important system characteristics such as bandwidth and phase behaviour can be evaluated. The separated transfer functions also simplify the tuning process and help to identify possible controller or filtering issues.

\subsubsection*{Estimation of the Closed-Loop Components}

By estimating different transfer functions from the measured data, the behaviour of the individual components of the control loop can be analysed separately. This allows the controller, plant and feedback paths to be evaluated individually instead of only observing the overall closed-loop response. As already described in the theoretical part, this approach simplifies the system analysis and improves the accuracy of the identified transfer functions.

The first step is to estimate the closed-loop transfer function from the reference input \( w \) to the measured output \( y \), which is denoted as \( G_{yw} \):

\begin{equation}
T_{\text{AltHold,meas}}(\omega)=G_{\text{yw}}(\omega) = \frac{Y(\omega)}{W(\omega)}
\end{equation}

where \( Y(\omega) \) is the Fourier transform of the measured output signal and \( W(\omega) \) is the Fourier transform of the reference input, including the chirp signal. These signals are filtered and processed as described in the theory chapter in order to reduce noise and improve the quality of the estimated frequency response.

The transfer function \( G_{\text{yw}} \) represents the overall closed-loop behaviour of the system and can be used to evaluate important characteristics such as bandwidth, phase behaviour and stability margins.

To estimate the plant transfer function, the transfer function from the reference input to the controller output \( u \) is also calculated:

\begin{equation}
G_{\text{uw}}(\omega) = \frac{U(\omega)}{W(\omega)}
\end{equation}

where \( U(\omega) \) is the Fourier transform of the controller output signal. This transfer function describes the dynamic relationship between the reference input and the control action applied to the plant.

To estimate the D-term of the controller, the transfer function from the reference input to the shared output of the PI controller and the D-term is additionally required. This transfer function is denoted as \( G_{\text{vw}} \):

\begin{equation}
G_{\text{ur}}(\omega) = \frac{U(\omega)}{W(\omega)}
\end{equation}

where \( V(\omega) \) is the Fourier transform of the signal after the PI controller and before the D-term feedback path. This additional transfer function is required because the D-term is implemented in the feedback path of the control loop and therefore cannot directly be separated from the remaining controller dynamics.

With these transfer functions, the plant transfer function can be estimated as

\begin{equation}
P_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega) = \frac{G_{\text{yw}}(\omega)}{G_\text{{vw}}(\omega)}
\end{equation}

This approach allows the estimation of the real plant dynamics, which can then be used for further analysis and controller tuning.

The transfer function of the PI controller can be estimated as

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{PI,meas}}(\omega) = \frac{G_{\text{yw}}(\omega)}{G_{\text{vw}}(\omega) - G_{\text{yw}}(\omega)}
\end{equation}

This estimation is required for the compensation of the I-Term Relax algorithm described in the theory chapter. By comparing the estimated transfer function with the analytical PI controller model, a compensation factor can be calculated and applied to both the original and the newly designed controller.

The D-term and its filter can be estimated in a similar way:

\begin{equation}
C_{\text{D,meas}}(\omega) = \frac{G_{\text{uw}}(\omega) - G_{\text{vw}}(\omega)}{T_{\text{AltHold,meas}}(\omega)}
\end{equation}

The estimation of the D-term was especially useful during the development of the tuning tool, since it helped to better understand the D-term behaviour and identify implementation issues.

With the estimated plant and controller transfer functions, the complete control loop can be analysed and the tuning method can be applied to optimize the controller parameters for improved performance.

\newpage

\subsubsection*{Implementation of the Tuning Method}

Based on the estimated plant and controller transfer functions, the tuning of the PID controllers can be performed. The goal is to adjust the controller parameters to achieve the desired tuning characteristics, such as stability, responsiveness and robustness.

The calculation of the tuning parameters for the altitude hold control loop follows the same approach as for the previously described control loops, but uses different control loop components. The relevant components are the estimated plant \(P_{\text{AltHold}}\), obtained from the measured data, and the altitude hold controller consisting of the PI controller \(C_{\text{PI}}\), the D-controller \(C_\text{d}\) and the associated PT2 filter with the newly calculated tuning parameters.

With these components, the transfer functions for the Gang of Four analysis can be calculated. First, the closed-loop transfer function of the altitude hold control loop is given by

\begin{equation}
T_{\text{AltHold}} = \frac{C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) P_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega)}{1 + P_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega)(C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_\text{d}(\omega))}
\end{equation}

The sensitivity function can also be calculated with the new controller as follows. As already discussed in the theoretical part, the  sensitivity function is important to evaluate the robustness of the system and the disturbance rejection behaviour:

\begin{equation}
S_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega) = \frac{1}{1 + P_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega)(C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_\text{d}(\omega))}
\end{equation}

The controller effort can also be calculated with the new controller as follows. This function can be used to evaluate the controller activity and the amplification of noise at higher frequencies:

\begin{equation}
SC_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega) = \frac{C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_\text{d}(\omega)}
{1 + P_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega)(C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_\text{d}(\omega))}
\end{equation}

Or the compliance function, which shows how disturbances acting on the plant affect the system output:
\begin{equation}
SP_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega) = \frac{P_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega)}
{1 + P_{\text{AltHold}}(\omega)(C_{\text{PI}}(\omega) + C_\text{d}(\omega))}
\end{equation}

These metrics allows us to evaluate the performance of the new controller parameters and make informed decisions about further adjustments to optimize the altitude hold control loop for improved stability, responsiveness and disturbance rejection. More details about that can be found in the next section, where the results of the tuning process are evaluated based on the step response and the Gang of Four plots.

\section{Position Hold Controller}

Position Hold is an autonomous flight mode that allows the aircraft to maintain its current position without continuous pilot intervention. The flight controller continuously evaluates the position of the aircraft and generates corrective commands to counteract disturbances such as wind or drift. Similar to the previously discussed flight modes, the controller is integrated into the existing control architecture and uses GPS measurements as feedback. The following sections explain the underlying control structure, coordinate transformation and required sensors in more detail.

\subsubsection*{Position Hold Control Loop}

The Position Hold controller represents the outermost control layer in the flight control architecture. Its objective is to maintain the aircraft at a desired geographic location by continuously evaluating the position error and generating corrective attitude commands. These commands are forwarded to the existing angle controller, resulting in a cascaded control structure.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{Control_Loop_PosHold.png}
    \caption{Control structure of the Betaflight position hold controller.}
    \label{fig:Position_hold_control_loop}
\end{figure}

Figure \ref{fig:Position_hold_control_loop} illustrates the signal flow of the Position Hold controller. The control loop begins with the GPS position estimate, which is compared with the target hold position to generate position errors in the North-East plane. Based on these errors, the controller calculates corrective commands using proportional, integral, derivative and acceleration feedback terms.

For each axis, the controller output is given by

\begin{equation}
u_{\text{axis}}
=
K_p d_{\text{axis}}
+
K_i \int d_{\text{axis}} dt
+
K_d \dot d_{\text{axis}}
+
K_a \ddot d_{\text{axis}}
\end{equation}

where \(d_{\text{axis}}\) denotes the position error. The velocity term is estimated from successive position measurements, while the acceleration term is obtained by differentiating the velocity estimate. Since GPS measurements are inherently noisy, both signals are filtered before being used by the controller.

The controller calculations are performed in the Earth-Fixed frame, where the position errors are expressed in the North-East directions. However, the inner angle controller requires roll and pitch commands in the aircraft Body Frame. Therefore, the resulting control vector is rotated according to the current yaw angle before being passed to the inner control loop.

The resulting body-frame commands are then used as attitude references for the angle controller, which generates the corresponding angular rate commands for the underlying rate controller.

\subsection*{Betaflight Firmware}
\label{sec:Position Hold Betaflight Firmware}

While the previous section described the general control structure, the Betaflight firmware introduces several implementation-specific features to improve stability, robustness and GPS-based position tracking performance.

Unlike the inner attitude controller, which operates at a fixed update rate of 100 Hz, the Position Hold controller is executed asynchronously and runs only when new GPS data becomes available.

To improve both position capture and stationary hovering performance, the controller operates in two distinct phases: the \textbf{stopping phase} and the \textbf{holding phase}.

The stopping phase is activated whenever the pilot deflects the control sticks while Position Hold is enabled. During this phase, only the proportional, derivative and acceleration terms contribute to the control output, while accumulation of the integral term is suppressed. Any previously accumulated integral contribution is gradually leaked away to prevent windup when the aircraft is brought back to the target position. To improve braking performance, the derivative contribution is multiplied by a factor of 1.6.

The controller continuously monitors both the raw velocity estimate and its filtered counterpart. When their signs differ, indicating a reversal of motion, the aircraft is considered stopped on that axis and the controller transitions into the holding phase. During initialization, the controller always enters the stopping phase first. However, if the control sticks remain centered, the transition into the holding phase occurs immediately and the derivative boost is never applied.

During the holding phase, all four feedback terms contribute to the control output. The integral term becomes active and accumulates position error over time, allowing steady-state position errors to be eliminated.

To reduce GPS-induced measurement noise, the velocity estimate is filtered using an adaptive first-order (PT1) low-pass filter whose cutoff frequency depends on the current GPS update interval. The acceleration estimate is obtained by differentiating the filtered velocity signal and applying a second adaptive PT1 filter.

Before the generated attitude commands are forwarded to the inner control loop, several safety limits are applied. First, the combined derivative and acceleration contribution is limited to a maximum magnitude of \(35^\circ\). This prevents excessively aggressive corrections during rapid position changes or when Position Hold is activated at higher speeds. If the limit is exceeded, the vector is scaled proportionally:

\begin{equation}
\text{if} \quad |\vec{u}_{\text{DA}}| > 35^\circ, \quad
\vec{u}_{\text{DA}} \leftarrow \vec{u}_{\text{DA}}
\cdot
\frac{35^\circ}{|\vec{u}_{\text{DA}}|}
\end{equation}

After the proportional and integral contributions have been added, the complete PIDA command vector is constrained to the configurable maximum tilt angle:

\begin{equation}
\text{if} \quad |\vec{u}_{\text{PIDA}}| > \theta_{\max}, \quad
\vec{u}_{\text{PIDA}} \leftarrow \vec{u}_{\text{PIDA}}
\cdot
\frac{\theta_{\max}}{|\vec{u}_{\text{PIDA}}|}
\end{equation}

Since the Position Hold controller operates only at the GPS update rate, typically below 10 Hz, while the inner angle controller runs at 100 Hz, a PT3 upsampling filter is used to interpolate the generated attitude commands to the higher controller rate. This provides smooth attitude references for the inner angle controller despite the low GPS update rate.

Finally, a sanity check continuously monitors the distance between the aircraft and the target position. The allowable distance is scaled dynamically with ground speed and corresponds to twice the distance the aircraft would travel within two seconds, with a minimum threshold of 10 meters. If this limit is exceeded, indicating a GPS anomaly or loss of position control, Position Hold is automatically disengaged and control is returned to the pilot.

\subsubsection*{Firmware Modifications}

To evaluate and tune the Position Hold controller, an exponential chirp signal (as described in \autoref{sec:System Identification Using Chirp Signals}) is injected directly into the position error during flight. By sweeping through a predefined frequency range, the position controller is forced into a controlled response, allowing its frequency-dependent behaviour to be measured.

As mentioned in \autoref{sec:Position Hold Betaflight Firmware}, the magnitude of the D+A vector is limited to 35 degrees. For system identification, this limitation introduces nonlinear behavior. To mitigate this, a lead-lag filter is applied to the chirp signal before injection, reducing its amplitudes at higher frequencies. This results in less acceleration of the drone and therefore a smaller combined D+A vector length. \autoref{fig:pidDA_limit} illustrates how the D+A magnitude can easily exceed the 35-degree limit during chirp excitation without the lead-lag filter and how the filter affects the magnitude of the vector.

To ensure linear controller operation throughout chirp measurements, the firmware automatically transitions the position hold state machine into the holding phase upon chirp alignment. This explicit transition is critical because it keeps the integral term fully active and accumulating (rather than leaking away during manual control transitions) and eliminates the 1.6x derivative boost that would otherwise corrupt the measured frequency response. Additionally, the control sticks must remain centered throughout the chirp sweep to prevent suppression of the integral term. These constraints ensure that all four feedback terms (P, I, D, A) operate consistently and predictably, allowing the measured drone response to reflect true linear dynamics.

To enable repeatable measurements, an automated heading alignment sequence precedes each chirp sweep. Upon activation, a proportional yaw controller rotates the drone to face true North (0 degrees heading). Once the heading error falls within tolerance, alignment is locked, controller state is reset and the first chirp phase begins on the East-West (longitude) axis. At 0-degree heading, this Earth-Fixed axis maps cleanly to the body-frame roll axis. After the first sweep completes, the second chirp excites the North-South (latitude) axis, corresponding to body-frame pitch. This alignment ensures that Earth-Fixed and body-frame dynamics are directly observable. The \texttt{active\_axis} flag controls which axis receives excitation: 0 for longitude (East-West, roll) and 1 for latitude (North-South, pitch), enabling separate system identification for each axis. Since both axes share identical PID parameters, the controller design is based on the less dynamic axis.

CLI commands were added to configure all chirp parameters including start and end frequencies, sweep duration, amplitude, yaw alignment gains and lead-lag filter frequencies via the Betaflight configurator. New Blackbox debug variables log all required signals for analysis.

\subsection*{Control Loop Calculation}

With the injected chirp signal and the recorded flight data, the frequency response of the Position Hold control loop can be estimated. The estimation follows the procedure described in the theory chapter, including signal preprocessing, filtering and spectral averaging. The resulting transfer function represents the complete closed-loop behaviour of the Position Hold system, including both the controller and the aircraft dynamics.

The estimated transfer functions provide insight into the dynamic behaviour of the system and allow key characteristics such as bandwidth, phase behaviour and stability margins to be evaluated. These results form the basis for the subsequent tuning and validation process.

\subsubsection*{Estimation of the Closed-Loop Components}

In Position Hold mode, the chirp signal is injected directly into the target position in the Earth-fixed frame. This differs from Angle and Altitude Hold modes, where the excitation is applied to attitude or throttle commands. The Position Hold controller operates on the position error rather than the absolute position itself. Consequently, the position error is the most relevant signal for system identification and is therefore logged directly from the firmware.

At the beginning of the measurement, the aircraft is assumed to be hovering at the target position. Under this condition, the position error is initially zero. Once the chirp injection starts, the target position is periodically displaced by the injected signal, causing the controller to generate corrective attitude commands in order to follow the moving reference.

Since the injected chirp signal directly defines the target position offset, the reference position can be written as

\begin{equation}
    x_\text{ref} = u_c,
\end{equation}

where \(x_\text{ref}\) denotes the target position and \(u_c\) the injected chirp signal. During flight, the aircraft does not perfectly follow this reference, resulting in a position error \(e\). The actual aircraft position can therefore be reconstructed from the measured position error as

\begin{equation}
    x = x_\text{ref} - e = u_c - e.
\end{equation}

This approach avoids the use of absolute GPS coordinates and directly utilizes the same signals that are used internally by the Position Hold controller. As a result, the identified transfer functions correspond closely to the actual firmware implementation.

Using the rotating-frame filtering approach described in \autoref{sec:System Identification Using Chirp Signals}, the closed-loop transfer function from the target position to the actual aircraft position is estimated as

\begin{equation}
    T_\text{pos}(\omega)
    =
    \frac{X(\omega)}
         {X_\text{ref}(\omega)}.
\end{equation}

In addition, the transfer function from the target position to the controller output \(u_\text{EF}\) is estimated as

\begin{equation}
    G_{uw}(\omega)
    =
    \frac{U_\text{EF}(\omega)}
         {X_\text{ref}(\omega)}.
\end{equation}

The first transfer function describes the closed-loop behaviour of the Position Hold system, while the second transfer function represents the relationship between the reference position and the controller output. Using standard block diagram algebra, the plant and controller transfer functions can then be recovered as

\begin{equation}
    P_\text{pos}(\omega)
    =
    \frac{T_\text{pos}(\omega)}
         {G_{uw}(\omega)},
    \qquad
    C_\text{PID}(\omega)
    =
    \frac{G_{uw}(\omega)}
         {1-T_\text{pos}(\omega)}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{Implementation of the Tuning Method}

The calculation of the tuning parameters for the Position Hold control loop follows the same approach as for the previously described control loops. For the Position Hold evaluation, the injected chirp signal is applied directly to the horizontal position setpoint, while the resulting aircraft position is reconstructed from the logged position error.

The relevant components are the estimated plant \(P_{\text{PosHold}}\), which was obtained from the measured data, and the updated controller \(C_{\text{PID,Pos}}\) using the newly calculated tuning parameters.

With these components, the transfer functions required for the Gang of Four analysis can be calculated. First, the closed-loop transfer function is


\begin{equation}
T_{\text{Pos}}(\omega) =
\frac{C_{\text{PID,Pos}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{PosHold}}(\omega)}
{1 + C_{\text{PID,Pos}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{PosHold}}(\omega)},
\label{eq:T_pos_gof}
\end{equation}

the sensitivity function is

\begin{equation}
S_{\text{Pos}}(\omega) =
\frac{1}{1 + C_{\text{PID,Pos}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{PosHold}}(\omega)},
\label{eq:S_pos}
\end{equation}

the controller effort is given by

\begin{equation}
SC_{\text{Pos}}(\omega) =
\frac{C_{\text{PID,Pos}}(\omega)}
{1 + C_{\text{PID,Pos}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{PosHold}}(\omega)},
\label{eq:SC_pos}
\end{equation}

and the compliance transfer function is

\begin{equation}
SP_{\text{Pos}}(\omega) =
\frac{P_{\text{PosHold}}(\omega)}
{1 + C_{\text{PID,Pos}}(\omega)\,P_{\text{PosHold}}(\omega)}.
\label{eq:SP_pos}
\end{equation}

\section{Drone Tuning}

The different control loops of the drone use different controller structures. However, the tuning methodology is largely the same for all of them. Therefore, this chapter focuses on the gyro controller. Once the tuning process is understood, the same approach can be applied to the remaining control loops.

The objective of the tuning process is to achieve a fast and responsive drone while maintaining sufficient stability and robustness. This is accomplished by combining time-domain and frequency-domain analysis methods.

This chapter also describes the filter tuning process using spectra and spectrograms. Since these filters are only used in the gyro loop, this part is limited to the gyro controller.

The filtering algorithms themselves were developed as part of the Project Thesis and are therefore not discussed in detail in this thesis. Instead, the focus is placed on their practical application and the resulting tuning process.

\subsection*{Filter tuning}

Before tuning the PID controllers, effective filtering is essential to obtain clean signals. The general aim is to use minimal filtering, as excessive filtering introduces delay. However, filtering is necessary to achieve proper system tuning.

After running the analysis tool and importing a log file, the command window displays the filter and PID settings that were active during that flight. The tool does not automatically copy these values to the code, so manual transfer is required.

The next step involves analyzing the spectra and spectrogram plots to assess filtering effectiveness. It is important to note that these plots reflect only the data recorded in the loaded log file and the settings active during that flight. Changing filter settings in the code does not affect the existing plots. To evaluate the effects of parameter changes, a new flight must be performed and logged with the updated settings.

\newpage

The \textbf{spectra} \autoref{fig:Spectra_tuning} shows the frequency composition of the gyro signals. Peaks in this plot point to vibration sources at specific frequencies.
Three key aspects should be analyzed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resonance Peaks:} If all three axes show a peak at the same frequency, a resonance in the frame or loose component may be present. This can be addressed with a notch filter or by securing loose parts properly.
	\item \textbf{Noise:} High noise levels indicate general vibration issues. Adjusting the D-term low-pass filter can mitigate this. Clean spectral lines indicate effective filtering.
	\item \textbf{Axis Sum:} The axis sum displays the overall vibration level across all gyro axes. A properly filtered signal drops quickly after motor frequencies (around 600 Hz) and remains low, indicating effective suppression of high-frequency noise and resonances.
\end{itemize}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{Spectra.png}
    \caption{Frequency spectra of the measured gyro signals.}

\textbf{Vertical lines} indicate fixed-frequency vibrations from frame resonances or loose components. Dynamic notch filters provide robust suppression, particularly when frequencies shift due to bent propellers or changing load conditions. Testing showed that 3 notches from 100 to 600 Hz improved filtering, though this introduces additional delay.

\textbf{Diagonal lines} show motor-speed-dependent vibrations such as propeller noise. RPM filtering effectively suppresses these by placing notch filters on motor fundamental and harmonic frequencies. With three-bladed propellers, the filter was configured for three harmonics, effectively removing diagonal lines as shown in \autoref{fig:Spectrograms_tuning}.

\textbf{Aliasing} occurs when sampling contains frequencies above half the sampling rate. A PT1 low-pass filter with cutoff not exceeding half the sampling rate prevents this. With a 2 kHz logging frequency, the filter was conservatively set to 800 Hz.

\textbf{D-term low-pass filter:} The D-term amplifies high-frequency gyro noise, requiring filtering to prevent excessive motor oscillations and overheating. A PT3 filter with 100 Hz cutoff was selected for its steep roll-off, aggressively suppressing noise while introducing minimal delay. This conservative starting point can be increased during tuning if motor temperatures remain acceptable.

\textbf{Yaw low-pass filter:} This filter suppresses high-frequency yaw noise. Since yaw typically experiences less vibration than roll and pitch, default Betaflight settings typically provide adequate performance.

\subsection*{Gyro PID Tuning}

The tuning tool provides two different approaches for tuning the PID controllers: the step response and the Gang of Four. Both methods are based on the estimated plant and controller transfer functions, allowing for a systematic evaluation of the tuning adjustments. The step response is a time-domain analysis that shows how the system reacts to a sudden change in the reference input, while the Gang of Four is a frequency-domain analysis that provides insights into the stability and robustness of the system across a range of frequencies. For beginners, the step response is often more intuitive and easier to interpret, while the Gang of Four offers a more comprehensive view of the system's behaviour, especially for more experienced users.

\subsubsection*{Plant Model Identification}

The foundation for effective PID tuning is understanding the system dynamics. The angular rate controller forms the innermost control loop and its plant dynamics serve as the fundamental reference for overall system behaviour. \autoref{fig:plant_gyro_tuning} shows the identified frequency response with magnitude, phase and coherence. The magnitude plot exhibits the characteristic $-20\,\text{dB/decade}$ slope at low frequencies, confirming the integrator behaviour expected from rotational dynamics. At higher frequencies, motor dynamics introduce additional phase lag and magnitude roll-off, reflecting the limited actuator bandwidth. The coherence plot indicates measurement quality: high values reflect reliable measurements, while degraded coherence indicates sensor noise and unmodeled effects. For controller design, regions of low coherence suggest limiting high-frequency action through filtering and moderate derivative gains.

\subsubsection*{Evaluation Gang of Four}

The Gang of Four is a standard set of four transfer functions that together fully characterize a feedback control system. These four plots represent the Sensitivity (S), Complementary Sensitivity (T), Controller Effort (SC) and Compliance (SP). By analyzing these plots, the stability of the drone, its ability to follow commands, its response to external disturbances and the workload on the motors can be assessed in one comprehensive view. This structured approach enables efficient iterative tuning and systematic evaluation of how PID adjustments affect overall system performance.

\autoref{fig:gangoffour_tuning_gyro} shows the Gang of Four for the tuned system.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{gang_of_four_roll.png}
	\caption{Gang of Four transfer functions of the identified system for the original and newly tuned controller parameters.}
	\label{fig:gangoffour_tuning_gyro}
\end{figure}

The tuning process demonstrated these plots to be invaluable for diagnosing and improving drone performance. While all four plots provide useful information, focus should be placed on three in particular:

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Sensitivity (S):
	      This plot shows how the system reacts to noise and reference commands. The peak value is a key indicator of stability. In our experience, a peak below 6 dB is ideal for a well-damped system. Higher peaks suggest the drone might oscillate or become unstable, especially during aggressive maneuvers. We noticed that reducing the peak often required balancing stability with responsiveness, as overly conservative settings could make the drone feel sluggish.
	\item Controller Effort (SC):
	      This plot indicates how much work the controller is doing to maintain stability and follow commands. High effort at higher frequencies usually means the system is reacting to sensor noise rather than real disturbances. This can lead to unnecessary motor activity, overheating and wear. Adjusting D-term gains and optimizing filters can reduce high-frequency effort while maintaining responsiveness.
	\item Compliance (SP):
	      Compliance measures how much external disturbances, such as wind or propwash, affect drone motion. Lower compliance indicates more effective disturbance resistance. Increasing P-term gains improves disturbance rejection, though excessive gains can cause oscillations during rapid throttle changes. The optimal balance must be found to maintain both stability and responsiveness.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection*{PID Tuning Summary}

The offline tuning framework is designed to enable PID parameters to be optimized without repeated flight tests. The process is guided by these principles:

\textbf{Plant Identification}

Each tuning iteration beginns by identifying the plant transfer function from a chirp flight conducted at the current parameters. This measurement captures how the drone responds to control inputs under the existing tuning, including environmental conditions present during that flight.

\textbf{Offline Prediction}

Once the plant has been identified, candidate tuning parameters can be evaluated offline without additional flights. Two complementary analysis tools are employed:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Step Response:} Time-domain analysis showing how the drone responds to a stick command. Desirable characteristics include adequate rise time, minimal overshoot and smooth curves without ringing. The analytical prediction can be compared with the measured baseline response to validate the model quality.
    
    \item \textbf{Gang of Four:} Frequency-domain analysis displaying four transfer functions that fully characterize the closed-loop system. The Sensitivity peak indicates stability margins and should remain within acceptable bounds. Compliance reveals disturbance rejection capability. Controller Effort exposes whether high-frequency noise is being amplified. These plots reveal tradeoffs between responsiveness and robustness across the control bandwidth.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{In-Flight Validation}

Once the predicted response is deemed acceptable, the new parameters are applied to the flight controller and validated through actual flight testing. Aggressive maneuvers, wind disturbances and motor thermal behavior should all be assessed to ensure the tuning remains stable and responsive under practical conditions.

\textbf{Tuning Philosophy}

No universally optimal tune exists. Different applications should reflect different operational priorities: performance-oriented applications may demand aggressive response, while stability-focused platforms should prioritize robustness. Throughout this work, emphasis was placed on robustness and disturbance rejection, a philosophy that accommodates practical imperfections including bent propellers and asymmetric mass distribution. This conservative approach proved more valuable than pursuing peak responsiveness.

For coherent handling feel, particularly during combined maneuvers, roll and pitch axes should be tuned symmetrically. Asymmetric tuning, while sometimes beneficial, can introduce unintuitive cross-coupling that degrades predictability.

\textbf{Incremental Workflow}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A baseline flight is established and the plant is identified at current settings
    \item The step response and Gang of Four are analyzed to assess current behaviour
    \item One parameter is adjusted at a time by a small increment
    \item The response is predicted offline and key metrics are verified to remain within acceptable ranges
    \item The new parameters are validated through flight testing
    \item \textbf{Before making substantial changes, a new chirp flight is performed to re-identify the plant}
    \item The process is repeated from step 2
\end{enumerate}

This iterative approach ensures that the system remains within the linear region around the identified plant, thereby maintaining prediction reliability.

\subsection*{Conversion from MATLAB to Python}

To make the project more accessible to the FPV community, we decided to migrate the existing MATLAB codebase to Python.
The first step was to select suitable Python libraries that offer similar functionality to MATLAB while maintaining code efficiency and readability.
After evaluating several options, we chose the following libraries:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{NumPy}: For numerical operations and array manipulations, analogous to MATLAB's matrix operations.
	\item \textbf{SciPy}: For scientific computing tasks, including signal processing functions comparable to MATLAB's capabilities.
	\item \textbf{Matplotlib}: For plotting and visualization, providing features similar to MATLAB's plotting tools.
	\item \textbf{Pandas}: For data manipulation and analysis, useful for handling tabular data as in MATLAB tables.
	\item \textbf{Control Systems Library (control)}: For control system analysis and design, offering functions equivalent to MATLAB's Control System Toolbox.
\end{itemize}
We selected Python version 3.12, as it is well-supported and compatible with the required libraries.
To validate these choices, we developed a prototype script that implements the core functionalities needed for the Drone Tuning Tool.
The script loads flight log data from CSV files, parses and caches the data efficiently and extracts relevant signals such as setpoints and gyro measurements.
NumPy and Pandas are used for numerical and tabular data handling, while SciPy provides signal processing utilities for windowing and frequency analysis.
The Control Systems Library is employed to estimate the system's frequency response and step response from flight data, replicating MATLAB's identification routines.
Matplotlib is used for visualizing raw signals, chirp responses and system characteristics.
The modular structure, including custom data classes and caching, ensures fast and reproducible analysis.
This approach demonstrates that Python and its scientific libraries are suitable replacements for MATLAB in the drone tuning workflow.
While Pandas is not strictly necessary for the prototype, it simplifies data handling and improves code readability.
However, to minimize dependencies and ensure cross-platform compatibility, we may consider removing Pandas in the final implementation.
The flexibility of Python also allows for easier integration with other tools and automation of data processing tasks, which is beneficial for large-scale log analysis.


\subsection*{Transferring the MATLAB Library to Python}

The core functionality of the Drone Tuning Tool resides in its library functions.
To ensure that the Python implementation is both reliable and consistent with the original MATLAB code, we systematically transferred each library function from MATLAB to Python.
For functions with simple numerical outputs, we used \texttt{np.testing.assert\_allclose} with appropriate absolute and relative tolerances, saving the function inputs and outputs from MATLAB and comparing them with the Python implementation.
For more complex functions, such as those involving state-space outputs, we developed dedicated test scripts to compare the behaviour of the MATLAB and Python versions across a range of scenarios.

The initial validation process was conducted using flight data from a single test flight.
While several core functions successfully passed the comparison tests with the MATLAB reference implementation, some functions still require further refinement.
Specifically, the functions \texttt{apply\_rotfiltfilt} and \texttt{downsample\_frd} exhibit deviations that exceed the specified tolerance thresholds when compared to their MATLAB counterparts.
Additionally, \texttt{calculate\_transferfunctions} requires revision in the dynamic gyro low-pass filter configuration, as the current implementation produces runtime errors under certain conditions.

For the ongoing development, a comprehensive testing framework is planned that will validate all library functions against flight data from multiple test flights.
This extended validation will provide a more robust assessment of the Python implementation's reliability and help identify edge cases that may not have been apparent in single-flight testing.
The systematic transfer and validation process established so far provides a solid foundation for completing the migration and ensuring consistency between the MATLAB and Python implementations in future work.

\section{Position Hold Controller}

The position-hold loop operates at the GPS update rate of approximately \SI{10}{\hertz}, which constrains the achievable bandwidth relative to the \SI{8}{\kilo\hertz} inner loops. The loop was identified by injecting an exponential chirp into the Earth-frame position error, following the procedure of \autoref{sec:System Identification Using Chirp Signals}. The same PIDA gains are applied to both horizontal axes, so the tuning represents a compromise between them. The baseline and proposed tunings are summarised in \autoref{tab:poshold_cases}.

\chapter{Discussion}

The four control loops examined in this work fall into two regimes that differ in the bandwidth of the sensor that closes the loop. The gyro and angle loops are closed on the IMU at \SI{8}{\kilo\hertz}, where the chirp identification is well conditioned across the excited band; the altitude- and position-hold loops are closed on slower navigation-sensor feedback, a fused barometer/GPS altitude estimate at \SI{100}{\hertz} and GPS position at approximately \SI{10}{\hertz}, where the available identification bandwidth is itself the limiting factor. The discussion is organised along this split: the attitude loops, where the method performs as designed, and the navigation loops, where it serves as a proof of concept.

Three observations recur across all four loops and are stated here once. First, the prediction rests on a linear, time-invariant approximation of the plant identified at a single tune, so its accuracy degrades as the gain step away from that baseline grows; within the tested range the agreement was close for single increments and loosened for larger steps. Second, this places the tool in the role of a guidance instrument for \emph{incremental} retuning: the plant should be identified from a flight at the current tune, one step predicted at a time, and the plant re-identified between steps when a larger change is intended. Third, the configurations under which the tool was validated here (the experimental airframe and firmware build, and the loop rates noted above) bound the range over which these observations should be transferred to other setups.

\section{Attitude loops}

In both attitude loops the chirp excitation produces a well-conditioned identification across the band, so the linear, time-invariant approximation is the only meaningful source of prediction error.

\subsection*{Gyro}

For the rate (gyro) loop this is the best case observed: the analytical prediction reproduced both the rise time and the overshoot of the measured response for each single-increment step (\autoref{tab:gyro_step}), the overshoot being captured here as tightly as the rise time rather than over-predicted as in altitude hold. The overshoot gap was slightly larger for the bigger bad \(\rightarrow\) moderate step than for the smaller moderate \(\rightarrow\) tuned step, consistent with the linear, time-invariant assumption holding only approximately and loosening as the gain change grows.

\subsection*{Angle}

For the angle loop the prediction reproduced the measured rise-time trend for both single-increment gain steps but consistently indicated a slightly faster response than was measured, the analytical-versus-measured gap in \autoref{tab:angle_step} being larger for the bigger relative step (\(P = 50 \rightarrow 80\)) than for the smaller one (\(P = 80 \rightarrow 100\)). The loop remained essentially overshoot-free across the tested range, so the transient shape was captured in every case and the residual error is confined to the predicted speed.

\section{Navigation loops}

In the navigation loops the limiting factor is no longer the linear approximation but the identification itself. The feedback sensors restrict the usable bandwidth to a narrow, low-frequency band, so the synthesised step response, which requires accurate magnitude \emph{and} phase across the full identification band, is the less reliable of the two comparisons, while the Gang of Four, reported as magnitude only at each frequency, is treated as the primary tuning artefact. Both loops are therefore presented as a proof of concept rather than a validated predictor.

\subsection*{Altitude hold}
\label{sec:discussion_althold}

The altitude-hold prediction is bounded on two sides. Its accuracy degrades as the gain step from the default tune grows (the overshoot gap in \autoref{tab:althold_step} widens from \SI{8.3}{} to \SI{15.0}{} percentage points) because the plant was identified at the default tune and larger gains push the airframe outside the small-signal hover envelope, with between-flight drift adding a baseline offset. In frequency, the joint coherence supports the comparison only over approximately \SIrange{0.3}{1}{\hertz} (\autoref{fig:plant_altitude_measured}); the exponential chirp under-excites the lowest decade and environmental drift, thermals and slow wind dominate the altitude signal below \SI{0.3}{\hertz}, so the low-frequency rise of the tracking and compliance curves in \autoref{fig:althold_gof} is an estimation artefact rather than a physical resonance. The sensitivity peaks in \autoref{tab:althold_gof_S} fall inside the coherent band and are unaffected.

Within that band the analytical overshoot in \autoref{tab:althold_step} exceeds the measured while the rise times agree to within \SI{0.07}{\second}; this directional gap reflects the approximate firmware-D-path model and the band-limited, phase-noisy step reconstruction, so it is best read as a model-derived upper bound, with the rise time the more reliable step-domain metric. Two caveats follow: the chirp amplitude must keep the actuator out of saturation, and the results were obtained on firmware build \href{https://github.com/InsaneBroccoli/betaflight/commit/898435640}{\texttt{InsaneBroccoli/betaflight @ 898435640}} with \texttt{USE\_ALTHOLD\_CHIRP} enabled, around a hover baseline.

\subsection*{Position hold}

The position loop is identified through GPS feedback at approximately \SI{10}{\hertz}, which bounds the usable bandwidth far below that of the \SI{8}{\kilo\hertz} inner loops and introduces roughly \SI{100}{\milli\second} of latency through the cascaded position-error, PIDA, and rate stages; the joint coherence rises above 0.8 only between about \SI{0.27}{\hertz} and \SI{3}{\hertz}, below which wind disturbance and GPS noise dominate the position-error signal. Within that band the analytical tracking response is consistent with the measured proposed tuning, and the step-response prediction is closer than the GPS-limited resolution might suggest: the predicted overshoot lies within about \num{3} percentage points of the measured value (\autoref{tab:poshold_step}), and the measured proposed flight remained stable throughout. The Gang of Four within the approximately \SIrange{0.27}{3}{\hertz} coherent band is the most trustworthy artefact, while the step-response values should be read as coarse indicators given the \SI{10}{\hertz} resolution. Closing the remaining gap would require higher-rate position estimates (for example, sensor fusion or optical flow) or restricting the excitation to the lowest part of the band where the GPS feedback is most reliable.

\chapter{Conclusion}

The goal of this bachelor thesis was to develop an offline tuning tool for the Betaflight flight controller, allowing users to optimize controller parameters based on recorded flight data. The tool was designed to be modular and extendable, enabling future additions of tuning methods for different control loops. The implementation was carried out in both MATLAB and Python, with a focus on maintaining consistency between the two environments.

Within this work, the existing tuning framework was successfully extended with support for the angle control loop. The developed tool is therefore able to tune both the gyro and angle controllers based on flight data. The results showed that the tool can predict the system response with good accuracy for moderate parameter changes, while larger changes lead to reduced estimation accuracy due to nonlinear effects. This provides FPV pilots with a structured and data-driven alternative to traditional trial-and-error tuning methods.

Additionally, proof-of-concept implementations for the altitude hold and position hold control loops were developed, demonstrating the potential for further extensions of the framework. At the same time, these experiments highlighted limitations and challenges of the proposed tuning approach. In particular, the low update rate and noise of the GPS measurements in the position and altitude hold control loops complicate accurate system identification and controller tuning. These limitations may require additional methods such as sensor fusion or more advanced filtering techniques in future implementations.

An important outcome of this work was the strong interest from the Betaflight and FPV community. Discussions within the Betaflight project regarding possible integrations of offline tuning methods, as well as feedback and feature requests from developers of existing tuning tools, demonstrated the practical relevance of the developed framework beyond the academic scope of this thesis.
